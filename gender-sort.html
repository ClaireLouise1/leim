Nouns & articles: gender and plurals

Spot the pattern

  • el libro · el perro · el ordenador · el avión · el problema
  • la casa · la mochila · la canción · la televisión · la ciudad

Look at the last letters of each word. Which endings go with el, and which go with la? One word in the first line looks like it's in the wrong group: which one? Think about it, then check.

Show me

Words ending in -o are usually masculine (el, un), and words ending in -a are usually feminine (la, una). Some endings are even safer: -ción / -sión and -dad / -tad are always feminine, and -or and -ón are usually masculine. The odd one out is el problema: words ending in -ma that come from Greek are masculine. Masculine and feminine are just grammar labels: a table (la mesa) isn't "a girl"!

The endings

EL · masculine-o: el libro, el zapato · -or: el ordenador, el color · -ón: el avión, el balón · -ma: el problema, el idioma, el programa · -aje: el viaje
LA · feminine-a: la casa, la silla · -ción / -sión: la canción, la televisión · -dad / -tad: la ciudad, la amistad

Ends in -e or a consonant? There's no rule: learn the word with its article. el coche, el tomate, el árbol, el reloj, el lápiz but la noche, la llave, la clase, la flor, la luz.

The famous exceptions

Ends in -a, but masculineEnds in -o, but feminine
el día (the day)la mano (the hand)
el mapa (the map) · el planeta (the planet)la radio (the radio)
el sofá (the sofa)la foto (short for la fotografía)

Watch out: el agua (water) is feminine, but it takes el because it starts with a stressed a sound: el agua fría, las aguas. And people often have two forms: el profesor / la profesora, el amigo / la amiga, el / la estudiante.

The articles

thea / some
masculine singularel libroun libro (a book)
feminine singularla casauna casa (a house)
masculine plurallos librosunos libros (some books)
feminine plurallas casasunas casas (some houses)

Mixed groups use the masculine plural: los padres = parents (mum and dad), mis hermanos = my brothers and sisters, mis tíos = my aunt and uncle, los chicos = the boys, or the boys and girls. Spanish also uses the article where English doesn't: Me gusta el fútbol (I like football), Odio los lunes (I hate Mondays). And two little words join up: a + el = al (voy al cine), de + el = del (al lado del parque), but a la and de la never change.

Making plurals

The word ends in…Do thisExamples
a vowel (-a, -e, -o, -á)add -slibro → libros · coche → coches · sofá → sofás
a consonantadd -esciudad → ciudades · árbol → árboles · flor → flores
-zz → ceslápiz → lápices · luz → luces · pez → peces
-ión, -ón, -ús with an accentadd -es, lose the accentcanción → canciones · avión → aviones · autobús → autobuses
-en (stress before it)add -es, add an accentexamen → exámenes · joven → jóvenes
-s, with no accent on the endno change (only the article)el lunes → los lunes · el paraguas → los paraguas · el cumpleaños → los cumpleaños

Why do the accents come and go? The stress stays on the same syllable: can-CIÓN → can-CIO-nes doesn't need the accent any more, but e-XA-men → e-XÁ-me-nes does.

Compare your languages: English only has "the", so gender feels strange. But Irish nouns are masculine or feminine too: an fear (the man) but an bhean (the woman): after an, a feminine noun changes its first letter. French has le / la, and German has three genders! Spanish is one of the easier ones: the ending usually tells you.
Exam tip: In Writing, article slips (una problema, el mano, los ciudades) cost accuracy marks, and examiners see them in every answer. Learn every new noun with its article, as one chunk: not "casa" but "la casa". Then check the words around it agree: la casa es pequeña, los perros son negros. In Listening and Reading, the article tells you how many: un billete is one ticket, unos billetes is more than one.
