CBAs explained, with examples

A CBA (Classroom-Based Assessment) is a project your child does in class, which their own teacher judges. Here's what CBAs are, this year's rules, and what a strong piece of work looks like in Irish, English, Maths and Spanish.

What is a CBA?

  • A project done in class, over a few weeks. It might be a talk, an investigation, a portfolio or a piece of writing. It's done in class time, usually over about three weeks, with guidance from the teacher.
  • The class teacher judges it. Teachers then meet to compare work and agree standards. This is called a Subject Learning and Assessment Review (SLAR) meeting.
  • No marks, no percentage. Your child gets one of four descriptors: Exceptional, Above expectations, In line with expectations, or Yet to meet expectations.
  • It doesn't change the June exam grade. The descriptor appears on the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement (JCPA), the school report students get after the Junior Cycle, beside the exam grades.
  • Normally there are two per subject. CBA 1 is in 2nd year and CBA 2 is in 3rd year. For now, students only have to do one (see below).
No "past CBAs". Unlike the June exam, there's no CBA paper to practise from. Students pick their own topic within the task the teacher sets. The best way to prepare is to understand what the teacher is looking for, called the Features of Quality, and to look at good examples. That's what the rest of this page does.

This year's rules (2026/27)

Students sitting the Junior Cycle in 2027 and 2028 must do at least one CBA per subject (the school decides whether to do one or two). The written Assessment Task is not examined, so the June exam is 100% of the grade. Two subjects have a fixed rule, so the speaking skills are always assessed:

Maths, English and most other subjectsThe school chooses. Most do CBA 1 in 2nd year.

How teachers judge a CBA

Each CBA has a short list of Features of Quality, a checklist of what good work shows. The teacher reads or listens to the whole piece and picks the descriptor that fits best overall ("on balance"). One weak spot doesn't sink a strong piece.

DescriptorIn plain English
ExceptionalShows all the features to a very high standard. Any flaws are small next to its strengths.
Above expectationsShows the features very well. The student clearly understands every part of the task.
In line with expectationsShows most of the features well. A good, solid piece of work.
Yet to meet expectationsFalls some way short of what the task asked for. Often the student had good ideas but didn't finish or develop them.
About our examples: every task and model answer below was written by Léim to show what the Features of Quality ask for. They're not real student work and they haven't been graded. Only your child's teacher (and the SLAR meeting) can award a descriptor. Your child should use them for ideas, not copy them: the work has to be their own.

Example CBA 2: a presentation about hurling, then questions

The taskPrepare a short presentation on a topic that interests you, then talk about it with your teacher and classmates.
Read the model presentation and conversation

An cur i láthair

Dia daoibh, a chairde. Is mise Aoife agus inniu beidh mé ag caint faoi spórt a bhfuil an-suim agam ann: an iománaíocht.

Imrím iománaíocht le mo chlub áitiúil le cúig bliana anois. Bímid ag traenáil dhá uair sa tseachtain, Dé Máirt agus Déardaoin, agus bíonn cluiche againn gach Satharn.

Cén fáth a dtaitníonn sé liom? Ar dtús, is spórt tapa, corraitheach é. Ar an dara dul síos, buailim le mo chairde ann. Agus ar deireadh, is cuid de stair na hÉireann é: tá an iománaíocht á himirt in Éirinn leis na céadta bliain.

Rinne mé suirbhé i mo rang. As fiche dalta, imríonn ochtar spórt Gaelach.

Mar fhocal scoir, molaim daoibh triail a bhaint as. Go raibh maith agaibh as éisteacht. An bhfuil aon cheist agaibh?

An comhrá

Múinteoir: Cén áit a n-imríonn tú ar an bhfoireann?
Aoife: Is cúl báire mé. Is maith liom é mar bíonn orm a bheith an-tapa.

Dalta: An bhfuil sé contúirteach?
Aoife: Níl sé ró-chontúirteach, mar caithimid clogad. Ach ghortaigh mé mo lámh anuraidh!

Múinteoir: Cad a dhéanfaidh tú an samhradh seo chugainn?
Aoife: Rachaidh mé go dtí campa iománaíochta, agus b'fhéidir go mbeidh mé ag traenáil foirne óga.

In English: Aoife talks about playing hurling with her local club for five years, why she likes it (fast, exciting, she meets friends, part of Irish history) and a class survey (8 of 20 students play a Gaelic sport). Then she answers questions about her position (goalkeeper), whether it's dangerous (helmets, though she hurt her hand last year) and her plans for the summer (a hurling camp, maybe coaching younger teams).

Why this works
  • It's a real conversation, not just a learned speech. She answers follow-up questions with a reason and an extra detail.
  • Different tenses: present (imrím), past (ghortaigh mé) and future (rachaidh mé).
  • Clear structure with linking phrases: ar dtús, ar an dara dul síos, ar deireadh, mar fhocal scoir.
  • A topic she really cares about, which makes it easier to keep talking naturally.
  • Engages the audience, with a survey of the class and an invitation to ask questions.

Example CBA 1: three portfolio pieces

  1. A video blog (2 minutes): "Mo lá sa Ghaeltacht", filmed on a phone, describing a day at an Irish college. (This covers the recorded piece.)
  2. A poster responding to a poem from the class's literature list: the student's own short poem in reply, with pictures. (This covers the literary text.)
  3. A blog post about their favourite TG4 programme, with an opinion and a recommendation.

Why I chose them (shortened): the vlog shows my spoken Irish improving. The poem was the piece I worked hardest on and redrafted twice. The blog shows I can give opinions, which I couldn't do in 1st year.

Example CBA 1: a persuasive talk, "Phones should sleep outside the bedroom"

The taskPrepare and deliver a 3-minute talk on a topic that matters to you. Use research and engage your audience.
Read the model talk (about 3 minutes)

Hands up if you checked your phone after eleven o'clock last night. … Keep them up if you were still on it at midnight. Okay, that's most of us, me included.

Good morning, everyone. Today I want to convince you of one simple thing: our phones should sleep outside our bedrooms.

First, the facts. Doctors say teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep a night. I surveyed our class last week. Out of 26 people, 19 said they use their phone in bed, and only 5 said they usually get eight hours' sleep. That's not a coincidence.

Second, it isn't just about time. The light from a screen tells your brain it's still daytime, so even when you put the phone down, your brain takes longer to switch off. And let's be honest: "one more video" is never one more video.

Now, I know what you're thinking: "But my phone is my alarm!" I thought that too. So for two weeks I tried an experiment. I bought a €6 alarm clock and left my phone charging in the kitchen. The first three nights were hard. By the second week I was falling asleep about half an hour earlier, and I didn't feel like a zombie in first class on Monday.

Some people say this should be a parent's rule. I disagree. If our parents take the phone, we just feel annoyed. If we choose to do it ourselves, it actually works.

So here's my challenge. Tonight, just one night, leave your phone outside your room. You won't miss anything that can't wait until the morning. And if you're worried about missing a message, remember: the group chat will still be there at 8am. It always is.

Thank you. Does anyone have any questions?

Reflection note (shortened)

Title: Phones should sleep outside the bedroom. Sources: a class survey (26 students), information on teen sleep from a health website, my own two-week experiment.

What I learned: starting with a question to the audience got everyone listening straight away. My own experiment was more convincing than the statistics. Next time: I'd slow down in the middle. I rushed the survey numbers and I'd put them on a slide.

Why this works (the Features of Quality)
  • Engages the audience from the first line (hands up), speaks to them directly and ends with a challenge and questions.
  • Clear purpose and shape: a hook, a clear opinion, three reasons, the other side answered, and a strong ending.
  • Uses real material: a class survey and a personal experiment, not just opinions.
  • Persuasive techniques such as rhetorical questions, humour and repetition, used naturally.
  • An honest reflection with a specific thing to improve.

Example CBA 2: one text from the collection, a short descriptive piece

The taskChoose two of your texts in different genres to be assessed. Write a reflection note for each.
Read the model text and reflection

"The Last Bus" (descriptive writing)

The 10.40 from the city was always late, and always cold. Tonight the heater coughed out air that smelled of dust and old chips, and the windows were wet with everyone's breathing.

A man in a high-vis jacket slept with his forehead on the glass, his hard hat balanced on his knees like a sleeping cat. Two girls in matching hoodies shared one set of earphones and laughed at something only they could hear. Behind me, a phone rang three times, stopped, and rang again. Nobody answered it.

Outside, the town slid past in pieces: the chipper's orange glow, a closed pharmacy with its green cross still flashing, a dog trotting home alone as if it had somewhere important to be.

When the bus finally hissed to a stop at the square, the man in the high-vis woke, looked around as if he didn't know where he was, and then smiled. He'd made it home. So had I.

Reflection note (shortened)

Genre: descriptive writing. Why I chose it: I wanted to show a place through small details instead of telling a story. What I learned: my first draft just listed things ("there was a man, there were girls"). In my redraft I used the senses (smell, sound) and comparisons ("like a sleeping cat"). Next time: I'd cut a few adjectives and vary my sentence openings more.

Why this works (the Features of Quality)
  • Control of the genre: it describes a scene and a mood without turning into a plot-heavy story.
  • Precise word choice: "coughed out air", "slid past in pieces", details from all the senses.
  • Shaped for the reader: a clear setting at the start, and a quiet ending that links back to the narrator.
  • The reflection shows real redrafting, which is the heart of this CBA.

Example CBA 1: How much water does a dripping tap waste?

The taskInvestigate a problem from everyday life using maths. Show how you defined the problem, the strategy you used, your calculations and what your answer means.
Read the model report (shortened)

1. The problem

The bathroom tap at home drips. My question: how many litres of water does it waste in one year if nobody fixes it?

2. Assumptions

The tap drips at the same rate all day and night. All the drips are about the same size. I ignore the days we're away on holidays.

3. Strategy and data

I counted drips for one minute, three times, at different times of day: 24, 26 and 25 drips. Mean = 75 ÷ 3 = 25 drips per minute.

To find the size of one drip, I collected 100 drips in a medicine syringe: 5 ml. So one drip = 5 ÷ 100 = 0.05 ml.

4. The maths

Per minute: 25 × 0.05 = 1.25 ml
Per day: 1.25 × 60 × 24 = 1,800 ml = 1.8 litres
Per year: 1.8 × 365 = 657 litres

5. Does it make sense?

A bath holds roughly 80 litres, so the drip wastes about 8 baths of water a year (657 ÷ 80 ≈ 8.2). That's a lot, but it's believable for a small drip that never stops.

6. Making it general

There are 60 × 24 × 365 = 525,600 minutes in a year. For any tap: litres per year = drips per minute × ml per drip × 525.6. Check: 25 × 0.05 × 525.6 = 657 ✓. My neighbour's tap drips 40 times a minute, so it wastes about 40 × 0.05 × 525.6 ≈ 1,051 litres a year (if its drips are the same size).

7. What I'd improve

The syringe only measured to the nearest 0.5 ml, so my drip size could be a bit off. Next time I'd collect 500 drips. Drip speed might also change with water pressure, so I'd count on more days.

Why this works (the Features of Quality)
  • A clear, focused problem with sensible assumptions written down.
  • A strategy that suits the problem, with data recorded carefully and repeated measurements averaged.
  • Accurate maths with clear units (ml, litres) and correct notation.
  • The answer is checked against real life (the bath comparison).
  • It goes further: a general formula that works for any tap.
  • Honest reflection on what was weak and how to improve it.

Example CBA 2: Do 3rd years spend more time on screens than 1st years?

The taskPlan and carry out a statistical investigation. Pose a question, collect and organise the data, and draw a conclusion that answers your question.
Read the model report (shortened)

1. Question

How does the time 1st years spend on screens on a school day compare with 3rd years in our school? I expect the answers to vary a lot within each year, so I'm comparing typical values and spread, not individual students.

2. Plan

An anonymous online survey: "Yesterday (a school day), about how many hours did you spend on a phone, tablet, computer, console or TV, not counting schoolwork?" To avoid bias I didn't only ask my friends: I picked 30 students from each year at random from the class lists (with the year heads' permission). I got 28 replies from 1st years and 27 from 3rd years.

3. Results

1st years (28)3rd years (27)
Median2.5 hours3.5 hours
Lower quartile – upper quartile2 – 3.5 hours2.5 – 4.5 hours
Range0.5 – 6 hours1 – 8 hours

I drew back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots and two box plots on the same scale. I used the median rather than the mean, because a few very high answers (6 and 8 hours) would pull the mean up.

4. Conclusion

A typical 3rd year spends about one hour more on screens than a typical 1st year (median 3.5 hours v 2.5 hours). The 3rd years' answers are also more spread out. But the groups overlap a lot: a quarter of 1st years spend at least 3.5 hours, the same as the typical 3rd year.

5. Limitations

The hours are students' own estimates, and people tend to under-guess screen time. "Yesterday" was one Tuesday in November. It might be different at the weekend or during exams. This is one school, so I can't say it's true for all Irish students. Next time I'd ask students to check their phone's screen-time setting for a whole week.

Why this works (the Features of Quality)
  • A good statistical question: it compares two groups and expects answers to vary.
  • A fair plan: a random sample, anonymous answers and one clearly worded question.
  • Suitable graphs and summaries, comparing both the typical value (centre) and the spread.
  • A justified choice of the median over the mean.
  • A conclusion that answers the question and goes beyond the numbers ("the groups overlap a lot").
  • Honest about limits: estimates, one day, one school.

Practise the maths behind these on Léim: ratio and rates, rounding and estimation, and the other Maths games.

Example CBA 1: a presentation comparing two festivals, then questions

The taskGive a short presentation on an aspect of the culture of a Spanish-speaking country, then answer your teacher's questions.
Read the model presentation and questions

La presentación

Hola, me llamo Seán. Hoy voy a hablar de dos fiestas: el Día de San Patricio en Irlanda y las Fallas de Valencia, en España.

Las Fallas se celebran en marzo, del 15 al 19. La gente construye monumentos enormes, las fallas, con muchas figuras que se llaman ninots. Algunas son muy graciosas. Todos los días, a las dos de la tarde, hay una mascletà: un espectáculo de petardos muy ruidoso. La última noche, el 19 de marzo, queman casi todas las fallas en la calle. Se llama la cremà.

El Día de San Patricio es el 17 de marzo, ¡en las mismas fechas! En mi pueblo hay un desfile por la mañana. Mucha gente lleva ropa verde y hay música irlandesa.

En mi opinión, las dos fiestas son muy divertidas, pero las Fallas son más ruidosas que San Patricio. Me gustaría ir a Valencia el año que viene porque quiero ver la cremà. ¡Gracias por escuchar!

Las preguntas

Profesora: ¿Qué hiciste el Día de San Patricio el año pasado?
Seán: Fui al desfile con mi familia y después comimos en casa de mis abuelos.

Profesora: ¿Qué fiesta prefieres?
Seán: Prefiero San Patricio porque es mi fiesta, pero creo que las Fallas son más espectaculares.

Profesora: ¿Hay algo negativo de las Fallas?
Seán: Sí, hay mucho ruido y mucha gente, y es difícil dormir.

In English: Seán compares Valencia's Fallas festival (15–19 March: giant figures, a daily firecracker show, and the burning of the figures on the last night) with St Patrick's Day (a parade, green clothes, Irish music). He says the Fallas are noisier and that he'd like to visit Valencia. Then he answers questions about what he did last St Patrick's Day, which festival he prefers and the downsides of the Fallas.

Why this works (the Features of Quality)
  • Culture: it compares Ireland with Spain, which teachers love to see.
  • A range of language: present, past (fui, comimos), "would like" (me gustaría), comparisons (más ruidosas que) and opinions.
  • Real interaction: the answers to the questions are full sentences with a reason, not one-word replies.
  • Clear and well organised, with a short introduction, two parts, an opinion and a thank-you.

How parents can help

Do

  • Ask what the task is and when it's due. CBAs happen in set weeks, and many schools post a CBA calendar.
  • Ask to see the Features of Quality. Your child's teacher will have shared them, and they're the checklist for a good result.
  • Talk through topic ideas. The best CBAs are about something your child genuinely cares about: a hobby, a sport, a local issue, a question at home.
  • Be the audience. Let them practise a talk on you, and ask them one or two questions afterwards, just like the teacher will.
  • Help them plan the weeks. Three weeks goes fast. Agree a mini-deadline for research, a draft and a practice run.
  • Encourage them to keep their notes. Research notes and drafts are part of the evidence in several CBAs.

Don't

  • Don't write it or rewrite it for them. The work has to be their own, and teachers can tell. A good reflection note depends on them having done the thinking.
  • Don't let AI write it. Check your school's rules on AI tools. Using them to write the work is a risk, and it robs your child of the practice.
  • Don't panic about the result. It's one line on the JCPA. It does not change the exam grade or CAO points.

Questions parents ask

Does the CBA count towards the Junior Cycle grade?

No. The exam grade comes from the June exam (100% of it for 2027 and 2028, because the Assessment Task isn't examined). The CBA descriptor appears separately on the JCPA.

When will we see the result?

Schools report CBA descriptors to students and parents during the Junior Cycle, and they're printed on the JCPA, which students usually get in the autumn after their exams.

What if my child is sick during the CBA weeks?

Talk to the school straight away. CBAs are done in class within national dates, so the school will know what's possible.

Can we query a descriptor?

Each subject's assessment guidelines explain how to query a result. Ask the subject teacher first, then the school.

What if my child studies a subject outside school?

They can't do that subject's CBA, so the JCPA shows it as "not reported". This doesn't affect the exam grade. See No Spanish at your school?
