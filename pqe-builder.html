Answering on texts: point, quote, explain

Almost every question on a text (an unseen extract, a poem, your novel, play or film) wants the same three things: a clear point that answers the question, a quote that proves it, and an explanation of how the words prove it. Some schools call it PQE, PEE or PIE: it's the same idea.

Spot the pattern

Answer 1: Aoife is nervous. She is waiting outside the music room and then she goes in and plays her violin and afterwards she is happy.Answer 2: Aoife is nervous before her audition. As she opens the case, “her hands trembled”. The word ‘trembled’ shows how anxious she is: her body gives her away.

Both answers make the same point. Which one would get more marks? What does it have that the other doesn't?

Show me

Answer 2. Answer 1 makes a point and then retells the story: there's no evidence. Answer 2 has a point (Aoife is nervous), a short, exact quote (“her hands trembled”) and an explanation that zooms in on one word (‘trembled’) and links it back to the point (anxious). That's PQE.

The three parts

💡 PointAnswer the question in your own words, in one sentence. Use the question's key word: “Cian is lonely at his new school.”
🔎 QuoteA few words, copied exactly, in quotation marks, built into your sentence: On his first day he “ate his lunch alone”.
🧠 ExplainZoom in on one word and say how it proves the point: “The word ‘alone’ suggests nobody wants to know him yet.”

Quoting well

  • Short: 2–6 words is usually enough. A whole line copied out wastes time and hides the key word.
  • Exact: copy the words as they are. “hands were shaking” is not the same as “hands trembled”.
  • Marked: put quotation marks around the writer's words, so the examiner can see what's yours and what's theirs.
  • Built in: When the ref gave a free, Saoirse “clenched” her fists. The sentence still reads well.

Explaining well

Useful starters: The word ‘…’ suggests … · This shows … · The verb ‘…’ makes … sound … · This tells us …. Three ways an explanation goes wrong:

  • Retelling: “This means her hands were trembling while she opened the case.” (It only says the quote again.)
  • Off the point: “‘Trembled’ shows she is angry.” (It argues a different point.)
  • Vague: “This is a good quote because it shows what she is like.” (Which word? Like what?)
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Everywhere you answer on a text: questions on an unseen extract (a story, a poem, a non-fiction piece), and questions on the novel, drama, poetry and film you studied. The questions say things like “Use evidence from the text”, “Support your answer with reference to…” or “Explain your answer”. Marks go to a clear point that answers the question, evidence (a short quote or a precise reference) and an explanation, not to retelling the story.

How marks are won and lost

  • Answer the question asked: use its key word in your first sentence.
  • One point, one quote, one explanation, then the next point. Two or three PQEs beat one long paragraph.
  • Quote briefly and exactly, in quotation marks.
  • Explain the words: name the word that matters and what it suggests.
  • Don't retell the story, and don't just drop a quote in with no explanation.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). The extract is original. "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

The Ferry Niamh stood at the rail as the ferry pulled away from Cape Clear. Her grandmother was a small shape on the pier, waving and waving. Niamh's throat tightened, and she gripped the cold rail until her knuckles turned white. Behind her, her little brother was already chattering about the chipper in Baltimore. She didn't turn round. She watched the island until the mist swallowed it.
  1. How does Niamh feel as the ferry leaves? Support your answer with one short quote.
    Show a model answer

    Niamh is sad to leave her grandmother. Her “throat tightened”, as if she is trying not to cry. The verb ‘tightened’ suggests she is holding back tears, so we can feel how hard the goodbye is for her.
    Where the marks would go: a clear point (sad / upset to leave); a short, exact quote; an explanation of one word that links back to the feeling.

  2. The writer says Niamh “gripped the cold rail until her knuckles turned white”. What does this tell us? Explain.
    Show a model answer

    It shows how tense and upset she is. Gripping something until your knuckles go white takes real force, so the writer shows her feelings through her body instead of telling us. ‘Cold’ adds to the sad, bleak mood of the goodbye.
    Where the marks would go: the feeling named; how the action shows it (show, don't tell); a comment on another word (‘cold’).

  3. Here are a student's point and quote: “Niamh's brother is not upset. He is ‘already chattering about the chipper in Baltimore’.” Write the explanation.
    Show a model answer

    The word ‘already’ suggests he has forgotten about the goodbye straight away, and ‘chattering’ makes him sound cheerful and carefree. This contrasts with Niamh's silence, so her sadness stands out even more.
    Where the marks would go: zooming in on a word (‘already’ or ‘chattering’); what it suggests; the link to the point (not upset), with credit for spotting the contrast.

  4. Choose a character from a novel or play you studied. Make one point about them, and back it up with a short quote and an explanation. Then add a second PQE.
    Show a model answer

    Plan for any text: (1) Point: “[Character] is [quality] in [this moment].” (2) Quote: 2–6 words you know exactly, in quotation marks. (3) Explain: “The word ‘…’ suggests …, which shows ….” (4) Second point on a different quality or moment, with its own quote and explanation. (5) A closing line on whether the character changes.
    Where the marks would go: two clear points that answer the question; accurate short quotes; explanations that zoom in on words; no plot retelling.
