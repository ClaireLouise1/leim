Functional writing: reports, reviews, articles & blogs

Functional writing does a job: it informs, reports, judges or persuades a real reader. Before you write, ask three questions: What type of text is it? Who is reading it? What does it need to do? The answers decide your layout, your opening and your tone.

Spot the pattern

The same news, four ways: the school's new skate park opens on Friday.

AOf the 120 students surveyed, 85% said they would use a skate park. I recommend that it opens at lunchtime.
BSkate Park Opens at Last
By Ellen Daly: Students in Carrick will finally get to skate at school from Friday, according to the principal.
CHi everyone! Guess what? Our skate park opens on Friday and I literally can't wait. Comment below if you're coming! #skatelife
DGood morning, everyone. Fellow students, on Friday our skate park opens, and it belongs to all of us. Thank you for listening.

Which one is a report, an article, a blog and a speech? What gave each one away?

Show me

A is a report: findings with numbers (“surveyed”, 85%) and a recommendation. B is an article: a headline, a byline (“By Ellen Daly”) and a source (“according to the principal”). C is a blog: chatty, talks to its readers, asks for comments, uses a hashtag. D is a speech: it greets a live audience and thanks them at the end. Same facts; the features change with the text type.

Five text types and their features

📊 Report“Report on …” title, terms of reference, findings (often numbers), recommendations, signed + dated. Formal, factual, often with headings.
⭐ Reviewdetails (title, director or author, price), a little plot (no spoilers!), praise, criticism, a verdict or star rating.
📰 Articleheadline, byline, an opening that answers who, what, where and when; facts and quotes; subheadings.
💻 Bloga catchy title and date, first person, chatty, talks to readers, asks for comments, links and hashtags.
🎤 Speechgreets the audience, signposts, rhetorical questions, ends with “Thank you for listening.”

How a report is laid out

Report on Lunchtime Litter at St Brigid's College 📋 Introduction (terms of reference)This report was requested by the principal, Ms Byrne, to find out why the yard is so full of litter, and to suggest solutions. 🔍 FindingsOf the 150 students surveyed, 90 said there are not enough bins. I found that the bins in the yard are full by eleven o'clock. 💡 RecommendationsI recommend that four more bins are placed in the yard and emptied at lunchtime. ✍️ Sign-offSigned: Aoife Byrne, Student Council Secretary, 12 May 2026

How a review works

ℹ️ Detailstitle, director / author, length, price, where to get it.
👍 Praisewhat's good, with a reason.
👎 Criticismwhat's weak, fairly.
⭐ Verdictstars or a score, and who would enjoy it.

Headlines

A good headline is short (usually under eight words), names the main fact and grabs attention: “Bees Close Bray Playground”. Headlines drop little words (the, a, is) and use the present tense. “Big News Today” tells the reader nothing.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

The writing questions on the paper often name a text type and a reader: write a report for the board of management, a review for the school magazine, an article for a local newspaper, a blog post, a speech. Marks go to writing that does the job: the right layout and features, the right tone for the reader, clear paragraphs and accurate English.

How marks are won and lost

  • Show the format from the first line: a report title, a headline + byline, a blog title and date, a greeting for a speech.
  • Match the tone to the reader: formal for a board of management, chatty for a blog.
  • Use the features: findings and recommendations in a report; a verdict in a review; quotes in an article.
  • Answer the task: if it asks for two recommendations, give two.
  • Don't write a story when the task asks for a report or an article.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. Your student council has asked you to write a report on lunchtime in your school. Write the title, the terms of reference and two findings.
    Show a model answer

    Report on Lunchtime at Scoil Mhuire, Carlow
    Terms of reference: This report was requested by the student council to find out how students spend lunchtime and what would improve it.
    Findings: Of the 120 students surveyed, 74 said the canteen queue takes more than ten minutes. Most first years said they have nowhere to go when it rains.
    Where the marks would go: a clear “Report on …” title; terms of reference saying who asked and why; findings that are factual, specific (numbers) and formal in tone.

  2. Write a headline and the opening paragraph of a newspaper article about a local teenager who has raised €5,000 for the hospital by swimming in the sea every day in January.
    Show a model answer

    Teen's Winter Swims Raise €5,000
    By Niamh Kearney
    A fourteen-year-old from Tramore has raised €5,000 for the children's ward at the local hospital by swimming in the sea every single day of January. Seán Power braved storms, hail and a water temperature of nine degrees. “Some mornings I nearly turned back,” he told our reporter.
    Where the marks would go: a short headline with the main fact; a byline; who, what, where and when in the first paragraph; a quote.

  3. Write the verdict paragraph of a review of a film, book or game you know, for your school magazine.
    Show a model answer

    Overall, this is a gripping, funny and surprisingly moving film. The middle drags a little, but the last half-hour more than makes up for it. I'd recommend it to anyone aged ten and up, especially if you like a good twist. Four stars out of five.
    Where the marks would go: a clear judgement; one strength and one weakness, fairly balanced; who would enjoy it; a rating; a lively tone for a school-magazine reader.

  4. The skate-park news in “Spot the pattern” has to go on the school blog. Name three features you would change from the newspaper article, and why.
    Show a model answer

    1. Swap the headline and byline for a catchy blog title and a date: “Our Skate Park Is Finally Here! (20 November)”. 2. Write in the first person and talk to the reader (“I can't wait, can you?”) instead of reporting facts from a distance. 3. End by inviting comments (“Tell us your best trick below!”) so readers join in.
    Where the marks would go: three clear changes; each linked to the blog's reader and purpose; an example of each.
