Vocabulary and dictionary skills

Good writers don't know more words than everyone else: they pick the exact word. These are the tools for finding it.

Spot the pattern

All of these mean “not”. Why does the prefix change?

impossible impolite immature
illegal illogical
irregular irresponsible
Show me

Look at the first letter of the root word. Before p or m, “not” is usually im-. Before l it's il-, before r it's ir-. That's why illegal has two l's and irregular has two r's: you keep the whole prefix AND the whole word.

Shades of meaning: the word ladder

Words that mean nearly the same thing can be weaker or stronger, smaller or bigger. Put them on a ladder: the weakest at the bottom, the strongest at the top.

🌡️ heatwarm → hot → boiling
😠 angerannoyed → angry → furious
😴 tirednesssleepy → tired → exhausted
🏃 speedwalk → jog → sprint

Swap “very” + a weak word for one strong word: very hot → boiling · very tired → exhausted · very funny → hilarious. Only use the strongest word when you mean it: “furious” about a lost pencil sounds silly.

Synonyms and antonyms

🟰 Synonymsame (or nearly the same) meaning
huge = enormous · begin = start
↔️ Antonymthe opposite meaning
huge ↔ tiny · ancient ↔ modern
📚 Thesaurusa book or site of synonyms. Check the meaning in a dictionary before you use a new one.

Prefixes and suffixes

Prefix = at the startun-, dis-, im-, in-, il-, ir- = not
re- = again · mis- = wrongly · pre- = before
Suffix = at the endit changes the word's job:
-ness, -ment, -tion → a noun
-ful, -ous, -ive, -able → an adjective
-ly → an adverb

Word families: one root, many jobs. Pick the one the sentence needs.

Dictionary skills

  • ABC order: same first letter? Look at the 2nd letter, then the 3rd: brand, brave, bread.
  • Guide words at the top of a page show the first and last word on it. fish … flag: “five” is on the page, “fly” is not.
  • Many words have more than one meaning (bat, bank, match). Read the whole sentence to pick the right one.
  • An entry tells you more than the meaning: how to say it, the word class (n, v, adj, adv) and an example.
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

There's no vocabulary section, but the words you choose count in every piece of writing, and questions often ask what a word or phrase suggests, or why the writer chose it.

How marks are won and lost

  • Precise words win marks: “she trudged home” tells the examiner more than “she went home”.
  • “What does this word suggest?” Give the meaning AND the feeling it creates.
  • Cut the “very”s: one strong word reads better than very + a weak word.
  • Don't use a word you're not sure of: a fancy word in the wrong place loses more than it gains.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. Replace each “very” phrase with one stronger word.
    The walk up Carrauntoohil was very hard. By the top we were very tired, very hungry and very cold, but the view was very beautiful.
    Show a model answer

    The walk up Carrauntoohil was gruelling. By the top we were exhausted, starving and freezing, but the view was stunning.
    Where the marks would go: one mark for each strong word that keeps the meaning (5). “Gigantic” for hard, or “furious” for tired, would change the meaning and lose the mark.

  2. Write the opposite of each word using a prefix: possible, legal, honest, regular, kind.
    Show the answer

    impossible, illegal, dishonest, irregular, unkind.
    Where the marks would go: one each, spelled right: keep the whole prefix and the whole word (il + legal = illegal).

  3. Fill each gap with a word from the family in brackets: “The (decide) was made (quick) because of the (dangerous) of the storm.”
    Show the answer

    The decision was made quickly because of the danger of the storm.
    Where the marks would go: a noun after “The”, an adverb to say how it was made, a noun after “the … of”.

  4. “The old man shuffled across the kitchen.” What does the word “shuffled” suggest about him? Why is it better than “walked”?
    Show a model answer

    “Shuffled” suggests he moves slowly, dragging his feet without lifting them, so he may be old, tired or unwell. It is better than “walked” because it shows us how he moves and makes us picture him, instead of just telling us he crossed the room.
    Where the marks would go: what the word means (slow, dragging steps), what it suggests about him (tired, frail), and why it works better than the plain word.
