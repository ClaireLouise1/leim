Junior Cycle English · Teacher pack

Speaking and listening

↗ Léim

Learning outcomes (2015 specification): OL1, OL2, OL3, OL4, OL5, OL6, OL7

Aims

By the end of the lesson students can open a short talk with a hook, use signpost words so listeners can follow, keep eye contact and a clear voice, reply to a speaker with a follow-up question, and agree, disagree, join in and ask for more politely in a group.

In this pack

p.1 Teacher notes · p.2 Discussion Sort cards (cut-up) · p.3 Talk Swap A/B (speaking + listening) · p.4 Wall poster · p.5 Worksheet: Fix the Talk + exam-style questions · p.6 Answers. Level: 1st year (good prep for the 2nd-year Oral Communication CBA). Time: about 60 minutes. All texts are original.

Procedure

5 minLead-in. Give two openings to the same talk: “Em, so, today I'm going to talk about sharks.” / “Sharks were swimming in the sea long before the dinosaurs.” Which one makes you want to listen? Why?
8 minGuided discovery. Elicit the three kinds of hook (question, fact, story) and the signposts for start, next point and end. Model bad habits for 20 seconds (head down, mumbling, swaying); students name each one and the fix.
10 minDiscussion Sort (p.2, groups of 4, one set of 24 cards). Sort into agree / disagree politely / join in / ask for more (6 each). Then deal the cards out and discuss “Should homework be banned?”: each student must use their cards, laying each one down as they say it.
15 minTalk Swap (p.3, pairs). A gives the one-minute talk; B listens without the text and fills the grid, then asks their follow-up question and gives one delivery tip. Swap.
17 minWorksheet (p.5). Fix the Talk in pairs, then the exam-style questions on "A Place to Skate" alone.
5 minExit ticket. Write a hook for a talk on your favourite place, and one question you would ask a classmate about theirs. Homework: play Talk Tips at leim.ie/talk-tips.html.
Errors to look for: announcing the topic instead of a hook · reading word for word · no signposts · "That's it" endings · follow-up questions that change the subject · disagreeing with "No, that's wrong". Support: the poster (p.4) on the desk; let weaker students hold their Talk Swap text and look up at each full stop. Fast finishers: rewrite the Talk Swap opening with a different kind of hook.

Each talk has a hook, three points with signposts and a strong ending. Can your partner catch them all without seeing the text?

Student A · Talk Swap

1. Give your one-minute talk. Read it through twice to yourself, then say it to your partner. Look up at least once per sentence.

"Why every teenager should learn to cook"
Who here can cook a proper dinner? Today I'll give you three reasons to learn. First of all, cooking saves money: a homemade pizza costs far less than a takeaway. Secondly, it's healthier, because you know exactly what goes in. Another reason is that it's a skill for life, because one day you'll live on your own. B. Exam-style questions: "A Place to Skate" (a talk to the town council)

Last summer, my friends and I were moved on from the shopping centre car park three times in one week. We weren't doing anything wrong: we just had nowhere to skate. Today I want to convince you that our town needs a skate park. First of all, a skate park would give young people a safe place to meet. Secondly, skating is exercise, and it gets us off our screens. On top of that, we would look after a place that is ours. In conclusion, you were all young once. Please give us a space to be young in.
  1. What kind of hook does the speaker use? Why does it work?
  2. List the four signpost words or phrases.
  3. Find two places where the speaker talks directly to the audience.
  4. Is the ending effective? Explain your answer.
  5. Write the opening of a talk on a change you would like in your school. Use a different kind of hook.

A. Fix the Talk

“Today I'm going to talk about sharks.”No hook. Start with a fact or a question: Sharks were here before the dinosaurs.
“Em, so, like, sharks are, like, fish.”Fillers. Pause instead of “em” and “like”.
Reads the whole talk from a page, head down.No eye contact. Use cue cards with key words and look up.
“That's it. Thanks.”Weak ending. Sum up and leave one last thought: To sum up, sharks need our protection, not our fear.
Friend: “I got a puppy!” Reply: “I want a new phone.”Changes the subject. Ask about it: What did you call your puppy?
“That's a stupid idea.”Rude. Disagree politely: I see what you mean, but I'm not so sure.

B. Exam-style questions

  1. A story: being moved on from the car park three times. It is personal and real, so listeners feel the problem before the speaker argues.
  2. First of all · Secondly · On top of that · In conclusion.
  3. "Today I want to convince you" · "you were all young once" · "Please give us a space" (any two).
  4. Yes: it sums up and speaks straight to the council with a memorable last line ("a space to be young in"), not "That's it".
  5. Credit a question or fact hook (since the model uses a story), a clear point of view and a plan.

Marking tip

For a written talk, reward a hook, audience awareness (you, we, a question), signposted points and a strong ending, more than length. Our guide, not an official marking scheme.
