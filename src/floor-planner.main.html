Area, perimeter & scale drawings

Spot the pattern

All three shapes have a base of 8 cm and a height of 4 cm. The rectangle's area is 8 × 4 = 32 cm².

Rectangle: 32 cm²
Triangle: ?
Parallelogram: ?

Imagine the triangle drawn inside the rectangle. How much of the rectangle does it cover? Now cut the triangle off the left end of the parallelogram and slide it to the right end. What shape do you get?

Show me

The triangle is exactly half of the rectangle: ½ × 8 × 4 = 16 cm². The parallelogram becomes the 8 × 4 rectangle: 32 cm². Notice that the sloping 5 cm side is never used: what matters is the perpendicular height (the dashed line at right angles to the base).

Perimeter or area?

  • Perimeter = the distance all the way round the edge. It's a length: m or cm. (Skirting board, fencing, a frame.)
  • Area = the space inside. It's in square units: m² or cm². (Flooring, carpet, paint, tiles.)
RectangleArea = length × width
Perimeter = 2 × (length + width)
TriangleArea = ½ × base × perpendicular height
ParallelogramArea = base × perpendicular height
Circle (disc)Circumference = 2πr = πd
Area = πr²

These formulas are in the Formulae and Tables booklet you get in the exam. Know where to find them, and know which length goes where.

L-shapes and other composite shapes

  • Find the missing sides first by comparing with the opposite side: 10 − 6 = 4 m and 7 − 4 = 3 m.
  • Area: the big rectangle minus the missing corner, 10 × 7 − 4 × 3 = 70 − 12 = 58 m². Or split it into two rectangles: 6 × 7 + 4 × 4 = 42 + 16 = 58 m².
  • Perimeter: add ALL six sides: 10 + 4 + 4 + 3 + 6 + 7 = 34 m. (For an L-shape this is the same as the rectangle around it: 2 × (10 + 7).)
  • A house end wall with a pointed roof = rectangle + triangle. A window with a curved top = rectangle + semicircle.

Circles and π

  • The radius r goes from the centre to the edge; the diameter d goes right across: d = 2r. Area uses the RADIUS: halve a diameter first.
  • Read what the question asks for π: the π button on your calculator, π = 3.14, π = 22/7 (handy when r is 7, 14, 21…) or in terms of π (leave π in: r = 6 → area 36π cm², circumference 12π cm).
  • Working back: circumference 18π → r = 18 ÷ 2 = 9. Area 49π → r² = 49 → r = 7.

Semicircle, diameter 6 m: area = ½ × π × 3² = 4.5π = 14.1 m². Perimeter = half the circumference + the straight diameter = ½ × π × 6 + 6 = 15.4 m. Don't forget the straight edge! A quarter circle has TWO straight edges (two radii).

Wheels: one full turn moves the wheel forward one circumference (π × d).

Real-life floors

  • Cost of flooring = area × price per m².
  • Packs: area ÷ what one pack covers, then round UP (27.5 ÷ 2.4 = 11.46 → 12 packs; 11 packs would leave a gap).
  • Tiles: get the units the same first. A 3 m by 2 m floor with 50 cm tiles: 300 ÷ 50 = 6 along, 200 ÷ 50 = 4 across, 6 × 4 = 24 tiles.
  • Skirting board = the perimeter minus the doorway.

Scale drawings and plans

  • 1 : 50 means 1 cm on the plan is 50 cm in real life. Plan → real: × 50. Real → plan: ÷ 50.
  • Watch the units: 7.4 cm × 50 = 370 cm = 3.7 m (÷ 100 for metres, ÷ 100,000 for km on a map).
  • Finding the scale: a 6 m wall drawn 3 cm long: 600 cm ÷ 3 = 200, so the scale is 1 : 200.
  • Real area from a plan: change each LENGTH to real life first, then multiply. A 9 cm by 7.2 cm room at 1 : 50 is 4.5 m by 3.6 m = 16.2 m². (Multiplying the plan's area by 50 is wrong: the area grows by 50 × 50.)
  • Squared paper: count the squares inside (area) or the square edges round the outside (perimeter), then use what one square stands for.
The classic mistakes: using the sloping side instead of the perpendicular height; forgetting the ½ for a triangle; using the diameter in πr²; forgetting the straight edge of a semicircle; leaving out the missing sides of an L-shape; adding instead of multiplying (or the other way round) for area and perimeter; writing m instead of m²; rounding packs down; mixing cm and m.
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Area and perimeter usually come in a real-life setting: a floor plan, a garden, a wall to paint, a window, a running track or a plan drawn to scale. The question builds up in parts: find a missing length, then an area or perimeter, then a cost, the number of packs or tins, or a comparison. Circles often ask you to give the answer in terms of π or to a number of decimal places, so read that instruction carefully.

How marks are won and lost

  • Write the formula, then put the numbers in, then the answer with its units (m or m²).
  • Show the missing lengths on the diagram before you add or multiply.
  • Use the perpendicular height, never the sloping side.
  • Follow the π instruction (π button, 3.14, 22/7 or "in terms of π") and round only at the end.
  • Packs, tins and tiles: round UP and say why.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. The new kitchen floor. Here is the floor plan of Niamh's L-shaped kitchen.
    (a) Find the lengths of the two sides that are not labelled. (b) Find the perimeter of the floor. (c) Find the area of the floor. (d) Flooring is sold in packs that each cover 2.4 m². Each pack costs €45. How many packs does Niamh need, and how much will they cost?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 5 − 3 = 2 m and 6.5 − 2.5 = 4 m.
    (b) 6.5 + 3 + 2.5 + 2 + 4 + 5 = 23 m.
    (c) 6.5 × 5 − 2.5 × 2 = 32.5 − 5 = 27.5 m².
    (d) 27.5 ÷ 2.4 = 11.46…, so she needs 12 packs (11 would not be enough). 12 × €45 = €540.

  2. Painting the end wall. The end wall of a house is a rectangle with a triangle on top.
    (a) Find the area of the wall. (b) The wall has a door 2.1 m high and 0.9 m wide that is not painted. Find the area to be painted. (c) The wall gets two coats of paint, and 1 litre of paint covers 8 m². How many litres are needed? (d) Paint is sold in 2.5-litre tins. How many tins should be bought?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Rectangle 8 × 5 = 40 m². Triangle: height 8 − 5 = 3 m, so ½ × 8 × 3 = 12 m². Total 52 m².
    (b) Door 2.1 × 0.9 = 1.89 m². 52 − 1.89 = 50.11 m².
    (c) Two coats: 2 × 50.11 = 100.22 m². 100.22 ÷ 8 = 12.53 litres (to 2 decimal places).
    (d) 12.53 ÷ 2.5 = 5.01…, so 6 tins: 5 tins (12.5 litres) is just short.

  3. Circles at home. A window is a rectangle with a semicircle on top.
    (a) Find the area of glass in the window, to 2 decimal places. (b) Find the length of the outside edge of the window, to 2 decimal places. (c) A circular rug has a circumference of 6π m. Find its area, in terms of π. (d) Aoife says the perimeter of a semicircle with diameter 1.4 m is π × 0.7 = 2.2 m. What has she forgotten? Find the correct perimeter, to 1 decimal place.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Rectangle 1.4 × 1.6 = 2.24 m². Semicircle radius 0.7: ½ × π × 0.7² = 0.7697 m². Total = 3.01 m².
    (b) Bottom 1.4 + two sides 2 × 1.6 + the curved top ½ × π × 1.4 = 2.1991: 1.4 + 3.2 + 2.1991 = 6.80 m.
    (c) 2πr = 6π, so r = 3. Area = π × 3² = 9π m².
    (d) She has forgotten the straight edge (the diameter). 2.1991 + 1.4 = 3.6 m.

  4. The plan. Seán draws a plan of his bedroom to a scale of 1 : 50. On the plan the room is 9 cm long and 7.2 cm wide. (a) Find the real length and width of the room, in metres. (b) Find the real area of the floor. (c) His bed is 2.1 m long. How long should it be on the plan? (d) Seán's friend works out the area of the room on the plan (64.8 cm²) and multiplies it by 50. Explain why this is wrong.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 9 × 50 = 450 cm = 4.5 m; 7.2 × 50 = 360 cm = 3.6 m.
    (b) 4.5 × 3.6 = 16.2 m².
    (c) 2.1 m = 210 cm. 210 ÷ 50 = 4.2 cm.
    (d) The scale is for lengths. Both the length AND the width are 50 times bigger in real life, so the area is 50 × 50 = 2,500 times bigger: 64.8 × 2,500 = 162,000 cm² = 16.2 m². Multiplying by 50 gives only 3,240 cm² (0.324 m²), far too small for a bedroom.
