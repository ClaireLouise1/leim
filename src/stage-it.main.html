Drama 1: how plays work

A play is written to be seen and heard, not just read. The script gives the actors their words (dialogue) and tells everyone how to bring it to life (stage directions). When you read a play, picture it on a stage: What do we see? What do we hear? How do the characters move and speak?

Spot the pattern

🅰️ORLA: (smiling, hugging her mam) I'm fine.
🅱️ORLA: (slamming the bedroom door) I'm fine.

Same two words. Is Orla fine in both? What changes the meaning?

Show me

In A, Orla really is fine: she's happy. In B she is clearly not fine: she's angry (or upset) and doesn't want to talk. The words are the same; the stage direction changes everything. In a play, what characters do often tells us more than what they say.

Reading a script

(A kitchen, late at night. Rain against the window. DAN sits at the table.)
NUALA: (entering, out of breath) Did you hear that?
DAN: (not looking up) Hear what?
🧑 Character namesin capitals, before each line.
💬 Dialoguethe words the actors say. No speech marks.
🎬 Stage directionsin brackets and italics: where we are, how to move, how to speak, what the face shows. Written in the present tense.

Upstage = the back. Downstage = nearest the audience. Stage left and right are the actor's left and right as they face the audience.

Five backstage jobs

🏠 Setthe place: backdrop, walls, furniture.
👗 Costumewhat the actors wear.
💡 Lightingspotlights, blackouts, a glow.
🔊 Soundeffects and music: thunder, a doorbell.
🧰 Propsobjects actors hold: a letter, keys, a cup.

Lighting and sound set the mood

👻 Spookydim, cold blue light · an owl, a creaking door
🎉 Happybright, warm light · cheering, lively music
😢 Sadsoft, grey light · a slow violin
🔥 Dangerflashing red light · a pounding drum
🌅 Peacefulgentle golden light · birdsong, waves

A blackout (all lights off) ends a scene with a shock. A spotlight on one character shows they matter most, or that they're alone.

Conflict drives every play

🥊 Person vs persontwo characters clash.
🧠 Person vs selfa fight inside: guilt, doubt, a hard choice.
🌪️ Person vs naturea storm, a flood, the sea.
🏛️ Person vs societyrules, laws, tradition.

A key scene is usually where the conflict comes to a head. To stage it, decide: where are they, what do we see, what do we hear, and how do they move and speak?

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Drama comes up in two ways: a studied drama question (a key scene, a character, conflict, what makes it work on stage) and sometimes an unseen extract from a play. Questions often ask you to imagine you are the director: how would you stage this moment?

How marks are won and lost

  • Use the stage directions as evidence, not just the dialogue: “(her hands shaking) shows she is frightened.”
  • Think like an audience: what do they see and hear? Say what the lighting, sound, set or costume adds.
  • Name the conflict and say who or what is in the way.
  • Give reasons for every staging choice: “a single spotlight, to show how alone he is.”
  • Don't retell the plot. Pick a moment and explain why it works on stage.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). The play is made up. "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

THE LAST FERRY (an extract from an imaginary play)
(A small ferry waiting room on Inis Glas, late evening. Rain lashes the window. A single bulb flickers. MAEVE, fourteen, sits on her suitcase. Her father, PEADAR, stands at the door, looking out at the sea.)
PEADAR: (without turning round) It's not coming, Maeve.
MAEVE: (jumping up) It has to come. School in Galway starts on Monday.
PEADAR: The sea doesn't care about Monday.
(A long pause. The wind howls. MAEVE crosses to the window.)
MAEVE: (quietly) You don't want me to go. Do you?
PEADAR: (turning at last, his voice breaking) I want you to go. I just don't want you to leave.
(Far off, a ship's horn sounds. The lights fade slowly to a single beam from the lighthouse.)
  1. What do the opening stage directions tell us about the setting and the mood? Use two details.
    Show a model answer

    We are in a small ferry waiting room on an island, late in the evening, in a storm. “Rain lashes the window” and “A single bulb flickers” create a tense, lonely mood: the weather is wild, and the flickering bulb makes the room feel cold and uncertain, like Maeve's future.
    Where the marks would go: the setting (place and time); the mood named; two details quoted from the stage directions, each linked to the mood.

  2. What is the conflict in this scene?
    Show a model answer

    There are two. On the surface it is person vs nature: the storm stops the ferry, so Maeve may miss the start of school. Underneath it is between Maeve and her father, and inside Peadar himself: he wants her to have her chance in Galway, but he can't bear to lose her (“I want you to go. I just don't want you to leave.”).
    Where the marks would go: the conflict named and explained; a quote; extra credit for seeing more than one layer.

  3. What does Peadar's last line, and the stage direction before it, tell us about him?
    Show a model answer

    “(turning at last, his voice breaking)” shows he has been hiding his face because he is upset; now he finally lets Maeve see. His line shows he loves her and is proud of her, but is heartbroken that she is growing up and leaving the island.
    Where the marks would go: a point about his character; the stage direction used as evidence; the line explained.

  4. You are directing this scene. Choose the lighting and one sound effect for the final moment and explain your choices.
    Show a model answer

    I would fade the room to near darkness, with one slow, sweeping beam from the lighthouse passing over Maeve and Peadar. It shows how small and alone they are against the dark sea, but the beam gives a little hope. For sound, the ship's horn, faint and far away: the audience wonders whether the ferry is coming after all, so the scene ends on a question.
    Where the marks would go: a clear lighting choice and a sound choice; a reason for each, linked to the mood or the characters' feelings.
