Junior Cycle English · Teacher pack

Media 1: advertising

↗ Léim

Learning outcomes (2015 specification): R9, R8, OL8, W3

Aims

By the end of the lesson students can say who an advert is aimed at (with evidence), name and explain six persuasive tricks, tell fact from opinion in an ad, judge a slogan and say what a picture suggests.

In this pack

p.1 Teacher notes · p.2 Trick Sort cards (cut-up) · p.3 Ad Swap A/B (speaking) · p.4 Wall poster · p.5 Worksheet: Fix the Answer + exam-style questions · p.6 Answers. Level: 2nd year. Time: about 60 minutes. All brands, names and places are made up.

Procedure

5 minLead-in. Read two ads for the same €12 water bottle (the game's "Spot the pattern"): A gives only facts; B has an Olympic swimmer, "Everyone on the team has one", "The best bottle ever made!", "Today only". Which makes you want it? Why?
8 minGuided discovery. Students find four tricks in Ad B. Name them: star power, bandwagon, exaggeration, hurry; add free gift and statistic. Ask: who counted the "9 out of 10"?
12 minTrick Sort (p.2, groups of 3, one set of 24 cards). Sort the lines into six groups of four. Each group picks the line most likely to work on them, and says why.
12 minAd Swap (p.3, pairs). A reads three ads like a radio ad; B says who each is for and the clue word; then swap. Finish with the quick-fire fact or opinion round.
18 minWorksheet (p.5). Fix the Answer in pairs, then the exam-style questions on the bike-hire advert alone.
5 minExit ticket. Write a slogan for a school tuck shop using alliteration or rhyme. Homework: play Ad Breaker at leim.ie/ad-breaker.html, or bring in a real advert and label its tricks.
Errors to look for: naming a trick with no quote or explanation · "it's aimed at people" with no evidence · treating anything printed in an ad as a fact · describing the picture instead of saying what it suggests · "it works because I like it". Support: the poster (p.4) on the desk. Fast finishers: design a poster ad for a made-up product using three tricks, then swap and spot them.

A. Fix the Answer

The ad uses a lot of persuasive techniques.Too vague. Name one, quote it, explain it: It uses star power ("Olympic champion Aoife Brennan"): readers trust a winner.
The ad is aimed at people.Name the audience and back it up: It's aimed at parents: "the school run", "your children".
The picture is nice.Say what the picture suggests: The family laughing on the zip line suggests a fun day out together.
"Delicious" is a fact because it's in the ad.Being in the ad doesn't make it a fact: "Delicious" is an opinion; nobody can prove it.
9 out of 10 dogs prefer Waggo, so it must be true.Question the statistic: We don't know how many dogs were asked, or who asked them.
The ad works because I like it.Give a reason linked to the audience: It works because the slogan is short and catchy, so teenagers will remember it.

B. Exam-style questions

  1. Teenagers: "Bikes for teens", "Everyone's cycling the coast road this summer", and the picture shows two teenagers.
  2. Fact: "€15 a day" or "Open Saturdays from 9 am". Opinion: "ride the wild coast!" (a claim nobody can check).
  3. Bandwagon ("Everyone's cycling…"): nobody wants to be left out. Hurry ("only 20 bikes left for July!"): readers book now before they stop to think. Also a statistic (20 bikes).
  4. Freedom, sunshine and fun with friends: an easy, exciting day out by the sea.
  5. Any reasoned view, e.g. yes: a short, catchy slogan with alliteration ("Pedal Power"), a clear price, and tricks that suit teenagers. Reward a clear opinion backed up with two quoted reasons.

Marking tip

Reward a trick named + quoted + explained over a list of names, and comments on the picture and layout as well as the words. Our guide, not an official marking scheme.
