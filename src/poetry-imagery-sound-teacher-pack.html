Junior Cycle English · Teacher pack

Poetry: imagery and sound

↗ Léim

Learning outcomes (2015 specification): R8, R11, R4, OL8

Aims

By the end of the lesson students can spot simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, onomatopoeia and rhyme, say which sense an image appeals to, and write a point that names a technique, quotes it and explains its effect.

In this pack

p.1 Teacher notes · p.2 Technique Sort cards (cut-up) · p.3 Poem Swap A/B (speaking + listening) · p.4 Wall poster · p.5 Worksheet: Fix the Answer + exam-style questions · p.6 Answers. Level: 1st year (useful revision in 2nd and 3rd). Time: about 60 minutes. All poems are original.

Procedure

5 minLead-in. Write three lines about the moon: The moon hung like a silver coin. / The moon is a lamp above our town. / The moon frowned down from behind a cloud. Which two compare? What's the one small word that's different? What can't a moon really do?
8 minGuided discovery. Elicit simile (like / as), metaphor (is), personification (a human action). Then say sizzle and fierce flames flickered aloud: what do students notice? Board the six techniques and the five senses, then model one "name it, quote it, explain it" answer.
12 minTechnique Sort (p.2, groups of 3–4, one set of 24 cards). Sort the cards into six piles. Then each group picks one card per pile and says what it makes them see or hear.
12 minPoem Swap (p.3, pairs). A reads "Saturday Morning" aloud twice; B listens with the book closed and writes the words for each technique. Swap for "The Match". Check together.
18 minWorksheet (p.5). Fix the Answer in pairs, then the exam-style questions on "Bonfire Night" alone.
5 minExit ticket. Write one simile and one metaphor about your school on a Monday morning. Homework: play Technique Spotter at leim.ie/technique-spotter.html.
Errors to look for: naming a technique with no effect · "it makes it more interesting" · simile and metaphor mixed up · one repeated word called alliteration · rhymes that only look alike (wood / food). Support: the poster (p.4) on the desk; give weaker groups the six pile labels. Fast finishers: write a 4-line poem about the weather with three different techniques.

B. Exam-style questions: "Bonfire Night"

The bonfire is a dragon in the dark,
its flames crackle, spit and spark.
Sparks rise like bees into the night,
and smoke hides every star from sight.
The air smells of burning wood;
our cold hands warm. The heat feels good.
  1. Find a metaphor in the poem. What picture does it create?
  2. Pick out two sound words. What do they help you hear?
  3. "Sparks rise like bees into the night": what is compared, and why is it a good comparison?
  4. Which senses does the poet appeal to? Give a short quote for two of them.
  5. Describe the rhyme in the poem. What effect does it have?

A. Fix the Answer

The poet uses a simile.Names it only. Add the quote and the effect: "Rain fell like tiny needles": the rain feels sharp and painful.
"The classroom was a zoo" is a simile.Wrong technique: no like / as, so it's a metaphor.
"The wind whispered" is personification and it makes the poem more interesting.Effect too vague. Say how: it makes the wind sound gentle and secretive, so the mood is calm.
"My boots go crunch, crunch, crunch" is alliteration.One word repeated is repetition. "Crunch" is onomatopoeia: it sounds like boots on snow.
"wood" rhymes with "food".They only look alike. wood / good rhyme; say them aloud.
The poet appeals to the senses: "Golden light glowed in every window and the bakery smelled of fresh bread and the stone wall felt rough and cold."Quote far too long. Use short quotes and name each sense: sight ("golden light"), smell ("fresh bread").

B. Exam-style questions

  1. "The bonfire is a dragon in the dark": it pictures the fire as huge, wild and fierce, breathing flames into the night.
  2. "crackle" and "spit" (onomatopoeia): the snapping and popping of the burning wood.
  3. Sparks are compared to bees: they are small, bright and fly up in a busy swarm.
  4. Sight ("smoke hides every star"), sound ("crackle, spit"), smell ("smells of burning wood"), touch ("our cold hands warm"). Any two with quotes.
  5. Rhyming couplets: dark / spark, night / sight, wood / good. The steady rhyme gives a warm, cosy, sing-song feel, like friends gathered round the fire.

Marking tip

Credit the technique (named correctly), a short accurate quote and a clear effect. The effect carries the most weight: "it makes it interesting" is not an effect.
