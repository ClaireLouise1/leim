Ask: words or numbers? Then: words in an order? Numbers counted or measured? Counted answers can't be in between (2.5 brothers); measured ones can (1.555 m).

2. A statistical question

One where the answers vary, so you need data: "How long do students in my class sleep?" Not: "How tall am I?" (one fixed answer).

3. Population, sample, census

  • Population: everyone you want to know about (all 650 students).
  • Sample: the group you actually ask (40 of them).
  • Census: data from every member of the population.
  • Random sample: everyone has the same chance of being picked (names from a hat, a random number generator).

4. Keep it fair (no bias)

  • Biased sample: asks only one kind of person (the gym, your friends, the choir).
  • Leading question: pushes an answer: "Don't you agree…?", "Surely…", "How much do you love…?"
  • Answer boxes: no overlaps, no gaps, a box for every answer: 0–2 · 3–5 · 6–8 · 9 or more.

5. Collecting the data

Primary data: you collect it. Secondary data: someone else did (Met Éireann, the CSO, a website).

Survey (ask people) · observation (watch and count) · experiment (change one thing, measure what happens) · existing data (look it up).

The four stages of an investigation (CBA 2):
1. Pose a question → 2. Collect the data → 3. Analyse the data → 4. Interpret the results.

6. Tally marks and frequency tables

Go through the data once, one tally mark per answer. Every 5th mark crosses the other four. The frequency is how many times an answer comes up; the frequencies add up to the number asked.

7. Grouped data

Lots of different numbers? Use classes of equal width: 0–9, 10–19, 20–29… Every value goes in exactly one class: 27 goes in 20–29, 30 goes in 30–39.

Exam tip: name the type of data with both words (numerical discrete, not just "numerical"), and when you say a sample is biased, say who is left out.
