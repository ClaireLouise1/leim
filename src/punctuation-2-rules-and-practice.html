Speech marks, colons and semicolons: the marks that make dialogue and longer sentences work. Every story you write needs them, and every answer is marked partly for accuracy.

1. Two ways to set out speech

Speech first"I'm starving," said Dara.
"Can I come too?" asked Liam.
Speech lastMam said, "Dinner is ready."
Dad asked, "Have you seen my keys?"

2. Four rules

1Only the spoken words go inside the speech marks.
2A capital starts the speech, even mid-sentence.
3The mark goes inside the closing speech mark. Speech first: a statement ends with a comma, and said / asked stays small after ? or !.
4New speaker, new line.

Single (' ') or double (" ") marks are both fine: be consistent. Also called inverted commas.

3. Direct and reported speech

Direct"I am tired," said Niamh.
ReportedNiamh said that she was tired.

Reported speech: no speech marks; that (or if for a question); I → she / he; am / is → was, are → were, have → had, can → could, will → would.

4. Colon : and semicolon ;

Colon : before a listI packed three things: a torch, a map and a flask of tea.
Not after are or include: The colours are green, white and orange.
Semicolon ; joins two full sentencesDara loves hurling; his sister prefers soccer.
A comma can't do this (a run-on). A full stop could.

5. Quotes in your answers

Put the exact words in quotation marks and keep them short: The word "raging" makes the sea sound furious. Poem and story titles go in quotation marks too.

Exam tip: a few short lines of well-punctuated dialogue bring a story to life. Check every closing speech mark: is the mark inside? Did a new speaker get a new line?
