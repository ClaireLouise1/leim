Speaking & listening: talking to a group

A good talk is easy to hear, easy to follow and starts with a hook. A good listener shows it.

Spot the pattern

A

“Em, so, today I'm going to talk about sharks. Em, they're, like, fish.”

B

“Sharks were swimming in the sea long before the dinosaurs. Today, I'll tell you three things about them. First of all…”

Same topic, same speaker. Which one would you keep listening to? What does B do in its first line? What does it promise in its second? Count the “em”s.

Show me

A announces the topic, fills gaps with em and like, and gives no reason to listen.
B opens with a hook (a surprising fact), tells listeners the plan (three things) and uses a signpost (First of all) so they always know where they are. A short pause works better than “em”.

Talking to a group

👀 EyesLook up and around the room, not at the floor or the page.
🔊 VoiceLoud enough for the back row. Stress key words.
🐢 PaceSlow down. Pause instead of saying “em”.
🧍 BodyStand straight, feet still, no fidgeting.
🗂️ NotesCue cards with key words, not a script to read.

Plan it: hook, signposts, ending

🎣 Hook❓ a question · 📊 a surprising fact · 📖 a short story
🚀 StartFirst of all · To begin with · Firstly
➡️ Next pointMoving on · Secondly · Another reason
🏁 EndTo sum up · In conclusion · To finish

Listening and discussing

👂 Listen wellNod, let them finish, then ask about what they said.
🤝 AgreeThat's a great point. · I agree with…
🙅 Disagree politelyI see what you mean, but… · With respect, I disagree.
🙋 Join inCan I come in here? · Could I say something?
🤔 Ask for moreCan you give us an example? · Why do you think that?
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Speaking is assessed in the Oral Communication CBA in 2nd year. In the written paper, you may be asked to write a talk or speech for your class or school, and the same skills win the marks: a hook, a clear plan with signposts, and words that speak to your audience.

How marks are won and lost

  • Open with a hook, not “Today I'm going to talk about…”.
  • Speak to your audience: “you”, “we”, “fellow students”, a question to them.
  • Signposts make your points easy to follow; each point gets its own paragraph.
  • End strongly: sum up and leave them with one last thought or a call to act.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. Write the opening two sentences of a talk to your class called “Why every school needs a garden”.
    Show a model answer

    “Have you ever eaten a strawberry you grew yourself? Today I want to convince you that our school needs a garden, for three reasons: our health, our planet and our exam stress.”
    Where the marks would go: a hook that speaks to the audience (a question), a clear point of view, and a plan the listener can follow (three reasons).

  2. Fill the gaps with signpost words: “____, a garden gives us fresh food. ____, it helps bees and birds. ____, every school should have one.”
    Show a model answer

    “First of all, a garden gives us fresh food. Moving on (or Secondly), it helps bees and birds. To sum up (or In conclusion), every school should have one.”
    Where the marks would go: a start word, a next-point word and an ending word, each in the right place.

  3. A classmate says: “I've just joined the camogie team.” Write two replies that show you are listening well.
    Show a model answer

    “That's brilliant! When is your first match?” · “What position are you playing?”
    Where the marks would go: both replies are about what she said (camogie, the team), both invite her to say more, neither changes the subject or turns it to you.

  4. In a class discussion, someone says: “Homework should be banned.” Disagree politely and give one reason.
    Show a model answer

    “I see what you mean, because we're all tired after school, but I'm not so sure. A little homework helps us remember what we learned that day.”
    Where the marks would go: you show you heard their point, you disagree without putting them down, and you give a clear reason.
