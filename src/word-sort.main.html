Parts of speech (word classes)

Every word in a sentence has a job. Knowing the jobs helps you write accurately and talk about a writer's language in the exam.

Spot the pattern

Try each test on the yellow word.

Can you put the in front?  the fox ✓  the slept ✗
Can you put they in front?  they slept ✓  they fox ✗
Can you put very in front?  very tired ✓  very slowly ✓

Tired and slowly both pass the "very" test. So what's the difference? Look at what each one goes with: the tired fox, slept slowly.

Show me

the ___ works → a noun.  they ___ works → a verb.
Tired describes a noun (the fox) → adjective. Slowly tells you how it slept → adverb. Many adverbs end in -ly.

The big four

📦 Nouna person, place, thing or idea
Maya, Cork, ball, courage
🏃 Verban action or a state
kicked, slept, is, seems
🎨 Adjectivedescribes a noun
the muddy ball · it was empty
⏱️ Adverbhow, when, where, how often
ran quickly · never came

The glue words

👤 Pronounstands in for a noun
I, you, she, it, we, them
📍 Prepositionwhere or when
under the oak · after dinner
🔗 Conjunctionjoins ideas
and, but, so, because, although

Same word, different job

A word's class depends on its job in that sentence: I run (verb) · a long run (noun) · a fast car (adjective) · she drives fast (adverb).

Stronger words

  • One strong verb beats a weak verb + adverb: ran very fast → sprinted.
  • Swap tired words: went, got, nice, good, big, very → bolted, leapt, delicious, gripping, enormous.
  • Adjective or adverb? He played well (not good). She sang beautifully (not beautiful).
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

You won't be asked to label grammar, but word choice earns marks all over the paper: questions on how a writer uses language ("pick two words that…"), and the accuracy and word choice in everything you write.

How marks are won and lost

  • Quote the exact word, then say what it shows. "The verb 'hammered' shows…"
  • Naming the word class (verb, adjective) makes your point sharper.
  • Strong verbs in your own writing: sprinted, not ran very fast.
  • Adjective for adverb slips lose accuracy marks: "played good" → played well.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. “Thunder growled over the hills. Rain hammered the tin roof, and the old shed groaned in the wind.” Pick out two verbs that make the scene feel threatening. Explain your choice.
    Show a model answer

    The verb “growled” makes the thunder sound like an angry animal about to attack. The verb “hammered” shows how hard and loud the rain was, as if it were trying to break in.
    Where the marks would go: one mark-earning point per verb: the exact word quoted + what it makes you see, hear or feel.

  2. Rewrite this sentence with stronger verbs and adjectives: “The man went down the nice street and looked at the big house.”
    Show a model answer

    The man trudged down the leafy street and gazed at the towering house.
    Where the marks would go: each weak word replaced by a stronger one that still makes sense.

  3. Correct the two mistakes: “She sang beautiful and the crowd clapped loud.”
    Show the answer

    She sang beautifully and the crowd clapped loudly. Both words tell us how something was done, so they must be adverbs.

  4. “The nervous boy whispered quietly.” (a) Name the word class of nervous, whispered and quietly. (b) Which word could you cut? Why?
    Show the answer

    (a) nervous: adjective · whispered: verb · quietly: adverb.
    (b) Cut quietly: “whispered” already means speaking quietly, so the adverb adds nothing.
