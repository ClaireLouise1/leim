Non-literary and visual texts

Not every text is a story or a poem. Non-literary texts are the ones you meet every day: posters, leaflets, adverts, reviews, reports, articles, blogs, websites, podcasts. Visual texts use pictures, charts, colour and layout to get the message across. The exam asks what they say, how they say it, and whether you can trust them.

Spot the pattern

A: The Greenway is 42 kilometres long.B: The Greenway is the most beautiful cycle in Ireland.C: The café opened on Main Street in 2019.D: The café makes the tastiest scones in Ireland.

Two of these you could check. Two are someone's view. Which are which, and which words gave it away?

Show me

A and C are facts: you could measure the Greenway or look up when the café opened (a number, a date). B and D are opinions: "most beautiful" and "tastiest" are judgement words. Someone else could disagree, and no one can prove them right or wrong.

Fact or opinion?

🧾 FactCan be checked or proved: numbers, dates, times, prices, places, what happened. "Tickets cost €8." "The library is closed on Mondays."
💭 OpinionSomeone's view: judgement words like best, worst, tastiest, amazing, boring, too, better, should, I think. "The show is a brilliant night out."

Adverts and reviews mix the two on purpose: a fact makes you trust the text, then an opinion slips in beside it. Ask: could I check this?

Reading a chart

  • Read the title and the axis labels first: what is being counted?
  • Read across from the top of each bar to the scale. Halfway between two lines = the number in between.
  • "How many more?" = take the smaller from the bigger.
  • In your answer, quote the numbers: "PE was the favourite, with 45 votes."

Parts of a poster or leaflet

  • Headline: big, bold words at the top to grab attention.
  • Picture (image): shows the event or product and sets the mood.
  • Caption: the small line under a picture that explains it.
  • When & where: day, date, time, place.
  • Contact details: phone, website, email: what to do next.
  • Also look at colour, font size and layout: what does your eye see first, and why?
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Almost every paper has a non-literary or visual text: a poster, a leaflet, an advert, a web page, a review, an infographic or a podcast transcript. Questions ask you to find information, tell fact from opinion, explain how the layout and images work, and say whether the text does its job (to inform, persuade or entertain). Marks go to answers that point to the text: quote the words, name the feature, give the number.

How marks are won and lost

  • Name the feature (headline, caption, chart, colour) and say what it does for the reader.
  • Quote or give the number: "40 volunteers filled 90 bags" beats "lots of people helped".
  • For fact or opinion, say why: "it can be checked" / "'best' is a judgement word".
  • Don't just describe the picture: say why it was chosen.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). The leaflet and the chart are made up. "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

Leaflet: Shannonside Swim Club JUMP IN THIS SUMMER! [photo of two girls laughing in the pool, caption: "Our junior squad at the Athlone Gala"] Lessons every Tuesday and Thursday, 5 p.m. to 6 p.m., Athlone leisure centre. Juniors €40 for a 10-week course. Last year 85 children learned to swim with us. Swimming is the best way to make friends and stay fit. Our coaches are the friendliest in the Midlands! Call Aoife on 090 555 0146 or visit www.shannonsideswim.ie Bar chart beside it: "Children in our club": 2023: 30 · 2024: 45 · 2025: 85.
  1. Write down two facts and one opinion from the leaflet.
    Show a model answer

    Facts: lessons are "every Tuesday and Thursday, 5 p.m. to 6 p.m."; a 10-week course costs "€40" (also: 85 children learned to swim last year). Opinion: "Swimming is the best way to make friends and stay fit": "best" is a judgement word, and someone could disagree (also: "the friendliest in the Midlands").
    Where the marks would go: two facts that can be checked; one opinion; a short reason for each.

  2. Look at the bar chart. How many more children were in the club in 2025 than in 2023? What does the chart suggest?
    Show a model answer

    85 − 30 = 55 more children. The chart shows the club growing every year (30 → 45 → 85), so it suggests the club is popular and doing well, which makes a parent more likely to trust it.
    Where the marks would go: the right number; a point about the trend, backed up with the numbers.

  3. How do the headline and the photograph try to attract young readers?
    Show a model answer

    The headline "JUMP IN THIS SUMMER!" is in capitals with an exclamation mark, and it talks straight to the reader with a command, so it sounds energetic and fun. The photograph shows two girls laughing in the pool, so a young reader imagines having fun and making friends, which matches the leaflet's message. The caption tells us they are "Our junior squad", so readers see children their own age.
    Where the marks would go: one point on the headline and one on the photo; name the feature, say what it does for the reader.

  4. Do you think the leaflet does its job well? Give two reasons.
    Show a model answer

    Sample: Yes. First, it gives every fact a parent needs (days, times, price, place and a phone number), so it is easy to sign up. Second, it mixes facts with persuasion: the chart and "85 children" make the club seem trusted, while the photo and "best way to make friends" make it sound fun. One weakness: it doesn't say what age "juniors" are.
    Where the marks would go: a clear opinion; two reasons, each pointing to the leaflet.
