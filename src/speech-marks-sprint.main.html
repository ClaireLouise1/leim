Punctuation 2: dialogue, quotes, colons & semicolons

In 1st year you learned commas, capitals and apostrophes (Comma Catch). Now for the marks that make dialogue and longer sentences work: speech marks, colons and semicolons.

Spot the pattern

A"I'm starving," said Dara.
"Can I come too?" asked Liam.
BMam said, "Dinner is ready."
Dad asked, "Have you seen my keys?"

Where do the comma, the question mark and the full stop sit: inside the speech marks or outside? When does the sentence use a comma, and when a full stop?

Show me

The mark that ends the speech always goes inside the closing speech mark. In A the speech comes first, so a statement ends with a comma (not a full stop), because the sentence isn't finished yet: said Dara. A question keeps its ?, and asked stays small. In B the speech comes last, so there's a comma after said, a capital to start the speech, and the full stop or ? inside the closing mark.

Four rules for speech marks

1. Only the spoken wordsgo inside the speech marks. said Dara stays outside.
2. Capital to startthe speech, even in the middle of a sentence: Mam said, "Dinner…"
3. Mark insidethe closing speech mark: ,"  ?"  !"  ."
4. New speaker, new lineEvery time someone new speaks, start a new line (a new paragraph).

Single (' ') or double (" ") speech marks are both fine: pick one and stick with it. Speech marks are also called inverted commas or quotation marks. A split sentence keeps going after the tag: "I know," said Ava, "but it's late." (small b: it's the same sentence).

Direct and reported speech

💬 Direct speechthe exact words, in speech marks:
"I am tired," said Niamh.
📝 Reported speechtells what was said: no speech marks, that (or if for a question), I → she, am → was:
Niamh said that she was tired.

Colon : and semicolon ;

Colon :introduces a list (or an explanation) after a complete sentence:
I packed three things: a torch, a map and a flask of tea.
Not after are or include: The colours are green, white and orange.
Semicolon ;joins two full sentences that belong together, often a contrast:
Dara loves hurling; his sister prefers soccer.
A comma can't do this (that's a run-on). A full stop could.

Quotation marks in your answers

When you quote a text in an answer, put the exact words in quotation marks and keep the quote short: The word "raging" makes the sea sound furious. Titles of poems and short stories go in quotation marks too: In "The Shop", the poet…

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

There's rarely a question that says "punctuate this", but every written answer is marked partly for accuracy: spelling, grammar and punctuation. Stories almost always need dialogue, and short, well-punctuated dialogue makes a story come alive. In answers on texts, quotation marks around your quotes show the examiner exactly which words you're using.

How marks are won and lost

  • Punctuate dialogue properly: speech marks, the mark inside, a new line for each new speaker.
  • Don't overdo it: a few short lines of dialogue are better than a page of it.
  • Use a colon before a list and a semicolon to link two ideas: it shows control of your writing.
  • Don't use a comma to join two sentences (a run-on).
  • Put quotation marks round every quote in an answer on a text.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

where is the ferry asked Ella it left ten minutes ago said the man at the pier ella dropped her bag on the ground what am I going to do now she groaned
  1. Rewrite the passage above with the correct punctuation, starting a new line for each new speaker.
    Show a model answer

    "Where is the ferry?" asked Ella.
    "It left ten minutes ago," said the man at the pier.
    Ella dropped her bag on the ground. "What am I going to do now?" she groaned.
    Where the marks would go: speech marks round only the spoken words; ? and , inside the closing marks; small asked / said / she after the speech; capitals for Where, It, Ella, What; a new line for each new speaker.

  2. Add a colon or a semicolon to each sentence: (a) The bag held two surprises a puppy and a note. (b) The morning was grey the afternoon was glorious.
    Show a model answer

    (a) The bag held two surprises: a puppy and a note. The colon introduces the list announced by "two surprises".
    (b) The morning was grey; the afternoon was glorious. Two full sentences that contrast, so a semicolon.
    Where the marks would go: the right mark in each; a short reason.

  3. Change this into reported speech: "I have never seen a puffin," said Conor.
    Show a model answer

    Conor said that he had never seen a puffin.
    Where the marks would go: no speech marks; that; I → he; have → had.

  4. Write four lines of dialogue between two friends who have just missed the bus. Use the punctuation rules from this page.
    Show a model answer

    "Run!" shouted Cian.
    "It's no use," said Ruth, stopping at the corner. "It's gone."
    "Mam is going to kill me," Cian groaned.
    "Not if we walk fast," Ruth said. "Come on."
    Where the marks would go: a new line for each speaker; the right mark inside each closing speech mark; capitals; a mix of tags placed before, after and in the middle; dialogue that moves the story on.
