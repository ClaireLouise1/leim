Narrative writing: your own story

Good stories are planned. A five-minute plan stops you running out of ideas halfway, or ending with “and then I woke up”.

Spot the pattern

🥅 The Nervous Keeper

1Once upon a time, there was a boy called Fionn who was afraid of the ball.

2Every day, Fionn hid at the back of the pitch during PE.

3But one day, the goalkeeper was sick and the coach pointed at Fionn.

4Because of that, Fionn had to stand in goal for the cup final.

5Until finally, Fionn saved a penalty and his team lifted the cup.

Read the first words of each line. What job does each line do? Which line is the problem, and which line solves it? Could you swap lines 3 and 4?

Show me

This is a story spine. Line 1 gives us who and their problem (Fionn is afraid of the ball). Line 2 is normal life. Line 3 is the event that changes everything. Line 4 is what happens because of it (you can have more than one “Because of that…”). Line 5 solves the problem from line 1: the boy who was afraid of the ball saves a penalty.
You can't swap 3 and 4: each line is caused by the one before. When you write the story, the spine words disappear and each line grows into a paragraph.

Plan in five minutes

  • 🌅 Once upon a time…Who is it about, where are they, what do they want or fear?
  • 🔁 Every day…Their normal life, before anything happens.
  • ⚡ But one day…One problem or event changes everything.
  • ➡️ Because of that…What it leads to. The tension builds.
  • 🏁 Until finally…The ending: solve the problem you set up.

While you write

✂️ One problemA short story has room for one problem and two or three characters. Cut events that don't belong.
🙋 First personThe narrator is in the story: I, we, my.
👀 Third personThe narrator tells us about others: he, she, they, a name.
🧭 Keep it steadyPick past or present tense, and I or he/she, and stick with it.
💬 DialogueA few lines of speech bring a character to life. New speaker, new line.
🏁 Pay it offThe ending solves the problem. No “it was all a dream”, nothing from nowhere.

For openings and twists, play Twist Detector. For description and feelings, play Show, Don't Tell.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

The Junior Cycle paper often asks you to write a story, or the opening or ending of one, linked to the theme of the paper. Narrative writing is also a good choice for your Collection of the Student's Texts in 3rd year.

How marks are won and lost

  • Plan first: a five-line spine takes five minutes and gives your story a shape.
  • Stick to the task: if it asks for a story with a title or a first line, use it.
  • Keep control: one problem, a few characters, the same tense and narrator all through.
  • Finish strong: an ending you planned from the start. Leave time to read it over.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. Plan a story called “The Storm” using the five steps of a story spine.
    Show a model answer

    Once upon a time, Orla lived with her gran on Achill Island and was terrified of the sea.
    Every day, she watched the fishing boats from the window but never went near the water.
    But one day, a storm blew in while Gran was out in the rowing boat checking her lobster pots.
    Because of that, Orla had to run to the pier in the rain and shine a torch so Gran could find her way.
    Until finally, Gran's boat bumped against the pier, and the next summer Orla learned to row.
    Where the marks would go: five clear steps, one problem, each step caused by the one before, and an ending that links back to the start (her fear of the sea).

  2. This opening keeps changing tense and narrator. Rewrite it in the past tense and the first person.
    I walked into the kitchen and the lights are off. Then she hears a scratching noise behind the fridge. I grab the sweeping brush and edged closer.
    Show a model answer

    I walked into the kitchen and the lights were off. Then I heard a scratching noise behind the fridge. I grabbed the sweeping brush and edged closer.
    Where the marks would go: every verb in the past tense (were, heard, grabbed), and the narrator is “I” all the way through.

  3. Here is a student's plan for a story called “The Lost Dog”. Which two events would you cut, and why?
    1. Max the dog runs off on Inch beach. 2. Mam buys a new car. 3. We search the sand dunes until dark. 4. A lifeguard hears barking in the dunes. 5. My cousin's birthday party is on Saturday. 6. Max is found asleep in a rowing boat.
    Show a model answer

    I would cut event 2 (Mam's new car) and event 5 (the birthday party). Neither has anything to do with finding Max, so they would slow the story down and take the reader away from the problem.
    Where the marks would go: the right two events, and a reason linked to the story's one problem.

  4. Write an ending (3–5 sentences) that solves the problem in this opening.
    The last ferry from Cape Clear left at six o'clock. At ten to six, Orla realised her little brother Cathal was nowhere to be seen.
    Show a model answer

    Orla raced back up the hill, shouting Cathal's name into the wind. She found him at the old harbour wall, crouched over a rock pool with a crab in his hands. “Come on!” she gasped, and they sprinted down to the pier together. The ferryman was holding the rope, grinning. “I wasn't leaving without the crab boy,” he said.
    Where the marks would go: the ending solves THIS problem (Cathal is found and they catch the ferry), it follows on from the opening, it builds a little tension, and it doesn't come from nowhere.
