Transformations & symmetry

Spot the pattern

S′ is the mirror image of triangle S in the y-axis. S has corners (1, 1), (4, 1) and (1, 3). Read off the corners of S′. What happens to the x-coordinate? What happens to the y-coordinate?

Show me

S′ has corners (−1, 1), (−4, 1) and (−1, 3). The x-coordinate changes sign and the y-coordinate stays the same: (x, y) → (−x, y). Each corner is the same distance from the mirror line as before, just on the other side. Can you guess the rule for a mirror in the x-axis? (It's (x, y) → (x, −y).)

The four transformations

A transformation moves every point of a shape. The new shape is the image (we often call it S′, "S dash"). All four keep the shape and size exactly the same.

Translation (slide)Every point moves the same distance in the same direction. The shape is not turned or flipped. In the picture, S → T: 6 left and 2 up.
Axial symmetry (mirror)A reflection in a line (the axis). Each point and its image are the same distance from the line, on opposite sides, and the line joining them is at right angles to it. The shape is flipped. S → A: in the x-axis.
Central symmetry (half turn)A reflection through a point (the centre). Go from the point to the centre, then the same distance again. It is the same as a rotation of 180°: the shape ends up upside down, but not flipped. S → C: in (0, 0).
Rotation (turn)Turn every point through the same angle about a fixed point (the centre of rotation). A quarter turn is 90°, clockwise or anticlockwise. S → R: 90° anticlockwise about (0, 0).

  • A mirror line m that isn't an axis: count the squares straight across to the line (at right angles to it), then the same number of squares on the other side. A point ON the line stays where it is.
  • Central symmetry in a point C: count how far you go from the point to C (across and up), then go the same again. For P(1, 4) and C(2, 1): 1 right and 3 down gets you to C, so the image is 1 right and 3 down again: (3, −2).
  • A translation given by two points (A → B, or an arrow): work out the move from A to B, then do the same move to every point.
  • Rotation of 90° about (0, 0): anticlockwise (x, y) → (−y, x); clockwise (x, y) → (y, −x). Easier than memorising: trace the shape and the axes, put your pencil on the centre and turn the page a quarter turn.

Axes of symmetry and centres of symmetry

An axis of symmetry of a shape is a line that folds the shape exactly onto itself (axial symmetry in that line leaves the shape where it was). A centre of symmetry is a point where a half turn fits the shape back onto itself.

Square
4 axes · centre ✓
Rectangle
2 axes · centre ✓
Rhombus
2 axes (the diagonals) · centre ✓
Parallelogram
0 axes · centre ✓
Kite
1 axis · no centre
Isosceles triangle
1 axis · no centre
Equilateral triangle
3 axes · no centre
Regular polygon, n sides
n axes · centre only if n is even

A rectangle's diagonals are not axes of symmetry: fold a sheet of paper along its diagonal and the corners don't meet. The square is the only rectangle where they are.

The classic mistakes: changing the wrong sign (the x-axis keeps x and changes y); mixing up clockwise and anticlockwise; thinking central symmetry flips the shape (it turns it 180°); moving a translation the opposite way (A → B, not B → A); counting to a mirror line m along the grid instead of straight across at right angles; saying a rectangle or a parallelogram has 4 axes; forgetting that a point on the mirror line (or the centre) is its own image.
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Transformations usually come as a diagram on a grid (often with the x- and y-axes) where you draw the image of a shape, write the coordinates of an image, or name the transformation that maps one shape onto another. Symmetry also turns up inside other questions: patterns, logos, letters and shapes, where you draw the axes of symmetry.

How marks are won and lost

  • Name it fully. "Axial symmetry in the y-axis", "central symmetry in (0, 0)", "a rotation of 90° clockwise about (0, 0)", "a translation 3 right and 2 up". "A reflection" or "a turn" on its own loses marks.
  • Move the corners one at a time, then join them up. Label the image (S′ or the letter asked for).
  • Use a ruler for lines of symmetry and the sides of an image, and draw axes of symmetry right across the shape.
  • Check the image: same shape, same size. Mirror images are flipped; translations aren't turned; half turns are upside down.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. Moving K. The quadrilateral K has corners (1, 2), (4, 2), (4, 4) and (2, 5).
    (a) Write the coordinates of the corners of the image of K under axial symmetry in the x-axis. (b) Find the image of the point P(4, 4) under central symmetry in the origin. (c) K is moved by the translation 5 units left and 1 unit down. Write the coordinates of the corners of the image. (d) Find the image of P(4, 4) under a rotation of 90° anticlockwise about (0, 0).
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Keep x, change the sign of y: (1, −2), (4, −2), (4, −4), (2, −5).
    (b) Change both signs: (−4, −4).
    (c) Subtract 5 from x and 1 from y: (−4, 1), (−1, 1), (−1, 3), (−3, 4).
    (d) (x, y) → (−y, x): (−4, 4). Check with tracing paper: P is in the top-right quarter and a quarter turn anticlockwise takes it to the top-left quarter.

  2. Name the move. Shapes A, B, D and E are all images of the triangle S.
    (a) Name the transformation that maps S onto each of A, B, D and E. (b) Which images are flipped over (mirror images of S)? (c) Ciara says D is the image of S under axial symmetry in the x-axis. Explain why she is wrong.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) A: a translation, 6 right and 5 down. B: axial symmetry in the y-axis. D: central symmetry in the origin (a half turn). E: a rotation of 90° clockwise about (0, 0).
    (b) Only B. A is just slid; D and E are turned, not flipped.
    (c) The image in the x-axis would stay on the left, below the x-axis: (−5, −1), (−2, −1), (−4, −3). D is on the right: its corners are (5, −1), (2, −1), (4, −3), which is what you get when both signs change. So D is the image under central symmetry in (0, 0).

  3. Complete the pattern. The line m is to be an axis of symmetry of this pattern.
    (a) How many more squares must be shaded? (b) Shade them (or describe where they go). (c) How many axes of symmetry does a regular hexagon have? Does it have a centre of symmetry? (d) Draw a quadrilateral with exactly one axis of symmetry and no centre of symmetry, and name it.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 5 more squares.
    (b) Each shaded square needs a shaded partner the same distance from m on the other side. Counting columns from the left and rows from the top, shade: column 8 row 1, column 5 row 2, column 7 row 3, column 6 row 4 and column 6 row 6.
    (c) 6 axes (3 through opposite corners, 3 through the middles of opposite sides), and yes, it has a centre of symmetry (a half turn fits it back on itself).
    (d) A kite (or an isosceles trapezium): one axis, and a half turn does not fit it back on itself.

  4. Two moves. The point A is (3, −2). (a) Find A′, the image of A under axial symmetry in the y-axis. (b) Find A″, the image of A′ under central symmetry in the origin. (c) Which single transformation maps A straight onto A″? (d) A translation maps (2, −1) onto (−1, 3). Find the image of (0, 0) under this translation, and the point whose image is (0, 0).
    Show the worked answer

    (a) A′ = (−3, −2).
    (b) Change both signs: A″ = (3, 2).
    (c) A(3, −2) → A″(3, 2) keeps x and changes the sign of y: axial symmetry in the x-axis.
    (d) The move is 3 left and 4 up. (0, 0) → (−3, 4). Working backwards (3 right and 4 down): (3, −4) maps onto (0, 0).
