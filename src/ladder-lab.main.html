Pythagoras' theorem

Spot the pattern

This right-angled triangle has sides 3, 4 and 5. A square is drawn on each side, split into little unit squares.

  • How many little squares are in the square on the side 3? On the side 4? On the side 5?
  • What do you notice about the two smaller squares and the big one?
  • Try it with a triangle that has NO right angle. Does it still work?
Show me

The squares hold 9, 16 and 25 little squares, and 9 + 16 = 25. The two smaller squares together make exactly the big square on the longest side. This works for every right-angled triangle and only for right-angled triangles: that's Pythagoras' theorem.

The hypotenuse

The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle. It is always the longest side. Find it first in every question.
Pythagoras' theorem (Theorem 14): in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse is the sum of the squares of the other two sides.
c² = a² + b²
It's in the Formulae and Tables booklet, but you need to know which side is c.

Finding a side

The hypotenuse: ADDLegs 5 and 12:
c² = 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169
c = √169 = 13
A shorter side: SUBTRACTHypotenuse 13, one side 5:
a² = 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144
a = √144 = 12
Then the square root√ at the end, every time. If it isn't a whole number, round as the question says: √52 = 7.211… = 7.2 (1 d.p.).
Sense check: the hypotenuse must be longer than both other sides, and a shorter side must be shorter than the hypotenuse. If your "shorter side" comes out longer than the hypotenuse, you added when you should have subtracted.

Square roots without a calculator

Learn the squares 1² = 1 up to 15² = 225. Then you can say where a square root lies: 50 is between 7² = 49 and 8² = 64, so √50 is between 7 and 8 (just over 7).

Is it right-angled? (Theorem 15, the converse)

If the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two, the triangle is right-angled, and the right angle is opposite the longest side.

  • 8, 15, 17: 8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289 = 17². Right-angled.
  • 6, 9, 11: 6² + 9² = 36 + 81 = 117, but 11² = 121. Not right-angled.
  • Pythagorean triples are whole numbers that work: 3, 4, 5 · 5, 12, 13 · 8, 15, 17 · 7, 24, 25 · 20, 21, 29. Any multiple works too: 6, 8, 10 · 9, 12, 15.
  • The builder's check: measure 3 m along one wall and 4 m along the other. If the gap is exactly 5 m, the corner is square. A longer gap means the angle is more than 90°, a shorter one less.

Pythagoras in real life

  • Ladders: the ladder is the hypotenuse, the wall and the ground are the other two sides.
  • Rectangles: a diagonal cuts a rectangle into two right-angled triangles. The diagonal is the hypotenuse (TV screens are measured along the diagonal).
  • Isosceles triangles: the height cuts the base exactly in half. Use half the base, not the whole base.
  • Journeys: north then east (or any two directions at right angles): the straight-line distance back is the hypotenuse.
  • Draw a sketch, mark the right angle, label the hypotenuse, then decide: add or subtract?

Why it's true

Draw the perpendicular from C to [AB]. It makes two smaller triangles, and each one is similar to the big triangle ABC (they share an angle and each has a right angle). Similar triangles have sides in proportion, which gives |BC|² = |AB| × |BD| and |AC|² = |AB| × |AD|. Add them: |BC|² + |AC|² = |AB| × (|BD| + |AD|) = |AB|². You won't be asked to write this proof out, but you should understand how it works.

Classic mistakes

  • Adding the sides (5 + 12 = 17) instead of the squares. √(a² + b²) is never a + b.
  • Forgetting the square root at the end (writing 169 instead of 13).
  • Adding when you need a shorter side: always subtract from the hypotenuse².
  • Using the whole base of an isosceles triangle instead of half.
  • Rounding too early: keep the full calculator value until the last step.
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Pythagoras turns up on both levels, often inside a bigger question: a ladder or a ramp, the diagonal of a rectangle, the height of an isosceles triangle before you find its area, the slant height of a cone, or a distance on a grid. Expect to find a missing side, to show that a triangle is right-angled using the converse, and to give an answer to a stated number of decimal places.

How marks are won and lost

  • Show the formula with the numbers in it. "x² = 6.5² − 2.5²" earns marks even if you slip with the calculator.
  • Round only at the end, and to the accuracy the question asks for.
  • Units. Give the answer in m or cm, as in the question.
  • "Show that" questions need the working. For the converse, write both sides out: 20² + 21² = 841 and 29² = 841.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. A ladder. A 6.5 m ladder leans against a vertical wall with its foot 2.5 m from the wall, on level ground. (a) How high up the wall does the ladder reach? (b) The top of the ladder slides down until it is 5.2 m above the ground. How far is the foot of the ladder from the wall now? (c) How far did the foot move?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) The ladder is the hypotenuse: h² = 6.5² − 2.5² = 42.25 − 6.25 = 36, so h = 6 m.
    (b) d² = 6.5² − 5.2² = 42.25 − 27.04 = 15.21, so d = √15.21 = 3.9 m.
    (c) 3.9 − 2.5 = 1.4 m.

  2. Is it right-angled? The triangle PQR has |PQ| = 20 cm, |QR| = 21 cm and |PR| = 29 cm. (a) Show that the triangle is right-angled. (b) Which angle is the right angle? Give a reason. (c) Seán says a triangle with sides 8 cm, 15 cm and 16 cm is also right-angled. Is he right? Show your working.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 20² + 21² = 400 + 441 = 841 and 29² = 841. They are equal, so by the converse of Pythagoras' theorem (Theorem 15) the triangle is right-angled.
    (b) ∠PQR: the right angle is opposite the longest side [PR].
    (c) 8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289, but 16² = 256. They are not equal, so no, it is not right-angled (8, 15, 17 would be).

  3. A tent. The front of a tent is an isosceles triangle with a base of 3.2 m and two equal sides of 2 m. (a) Find the height of the tent. (b) Find the area of the front of the tent.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) The height cuts the base in half: 3.2 ÷ 2 = 1.6 m. h² = 2² − 1.6² = 4 − 2.56 = 1.44, so h = 1.2 m.
    (b) Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 3.2 × 1.2 = 1.92 m².

  4. A shortcut. A rectangular field is 80 m long and 60 m wide. (a) Ciara walks along two sides of the field, from one corner to the opposite corner. How far does she walk? (b) Seán walks straight across the diagonal. How far does he walk? (c) Aoife worked out (b) like this: "60 + 80 = 140, √140 = 11.8 m". What did she do wrong?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 80 + 60 = 140 m.
    (b) d² = 80² + 60² = 6400 + 3600 = 10,000, so d = √10,000 = 100 m. (Seán saves 40 m.)
    (c) She added the sides instead of the squares of the sides. Her answer can't be right: a diagonal of 11.8 m is shorter than both sides of the field.
