Factorising

Factorising is expanding backwards: you start with x² + 5x + 6 and put the brackets back, (x + 2)(x + 3). You can always check your answer by multiplying it out again.

Spot the pattern

Here are some brackets that have been multiplied out. Look at the two numbers in the brackets and the last two numbers in the answer:

  • (x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 5x + 6
  • (x + 1)(x + 6) = x² + 7x + 6
  • (x − 2)(x − 3) = x² − 5x + 6
  • (x + 6)(x − 1) = x² + 5x − 6

Now go backwards. Can you put the brackets back on x² + 8x + 12? On x² − x − 12?

Show me

The two numbers in the brackets multiply to give the last number and add to give the middle number. For x² + 8x + 12 you want two numbers that multiply to 12 and add to 8. List the pairs that make 12:

1 and 12 → 132 and 6 → 8 ✓3 and 4 → 7−2 and −6 → −8

So x² + 8x + 12 = (x + 2)(x + 6). For x² − x − 12: multiply to −12, add to −1 → 3 and −4, so (x + 3)(x − 4). Check: x² − 4x + 3x − 12 = x² − x − 12 ✓.

1. Take out the highest common factor (HCF)

Always look for this first. Find the biggest number and the letters that are in every term (each letter with its smallest power), put it outside, and divide each term by it.

  • 6x + 9: HCF = 3. 6x ÷ 3 = 2x, 9 ÷ 3 = 3. So 3(2x + 3).
  • 4x² − 10x: HCF = 2x. 4x² ÷ 2x = 2x, −10x ÷ 2x = −5. So 2x(2x − 5).
  • 6ab + 9ac − 3a: HCF = 3a. So 3a(2b + 3c − 1). Don't lose the 1: 3a ÷ 3a = 1.
  • The HCF of 12x²y and 18xy² is 6xy: 6 is the HCF of 12 and 18, x is in both (smallest power x¹), y is in both (smallest power y¹).
Fully factorised means nothing more can come out. 4x² − 10x = 2(2x² − 5x) is true, but 2x² − 5x still has an x in both terms, so it is not fully factorised. The full answer is 2x(2x − 5).

2. Grouping (four terms)

Split the four terms into two pairs. Take a common factor out of each pair. The same bracket appears twice: take it out.

xy − 3x + 2y − 6
= x(y − 3) + 2(y − 3)
= (y − 3)(x + 2)
  • Letters only: ax − ay + bx − by = a(x − y) + b(x − y) = (x − y)(a + b).
  • If the second pair starts with a minus, take out the minus too: 2ab + 8a − 3b − 12 = 2a(b + 4) − 3(b + 4) = (b + 4)(2a − 3). Check: −3 × 4 = −12 ✓.
  • The two brackets must be exactly the same. If they aren't, check the signs in the second pair.

3. Quadratic trinomials: x² + bx + c

Find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. Then x² + bx + c = (x + first)(x + second).

  • Signs help: c positive → both numbers have the same sign as b. c negative → one plus, one minus (the bigger one takes the sign of b).
  • x² + 7x + 10: 2 × 5 = 10, 2 + 5 = 7 → (x + 2)(x + 5).
  • x² − 9x + 14: −2 × −7 = 14, −2 + −7 = −9 → (x − 2)(x − 7).
  • x² + 2x − 15: 5 × −3 = −15, 5 + −3 = 2 → (x + 5)(x − 3).

The area model backwards: the four boxes of (x + 5)(x − 3) are x², −3x, 5x and −15. Put x² and −15 in opposite corners, split the middle term into two boxes that work, and read the sides.

x² + 2x − 15 = (x + 5)(x − 3)

Higher Level: ax² + bx + c. Try pairs of brackets that give the first term and the last term, and check the middle. 2x² + 7x + 3: 2x² = 2x × x, 3 = 1 × 3. Try (2x + 1)(x + 3): middle = 2x × 3 + 1 × x = 7x ✓. So 2x² + 7x + 3 = (2x + 1)(x + 3). (Swapping the numbers, (2x + 3)(x + 1), gives 5x: wrong.)
Common factor first: 3x² + 15x + 18 = 3(x² + 5x + 6) = 3(x + 2)(x + 3).

4. The difference of two squares

A square minus a square: a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). The middle terms cancel when you multiply out.

  • x² − 49 = x² − 7² = (x − 7)(x + 7).
  • 2x² − 18 = 2(x² − 9) = 2(x − 3)(x + 3). Common factor first.
  • Higher Level: 9x² − 4y² = (3x)² − (2y)² = (3x − 2y)(3x + 2y).
  • No calculator: 53² − 47² = (53 − 47)(53 + 47) = 6 × 100 = 600.
  • x² + 49 is NOT a difference of two squares (it's a sum), and x² − 12 isn't either (12 isn't a square number).
Which method? 1. Common factor first, always. 2. Two terms with a minus and both squares → difference of two squares. 3. Three terms → x² + bx + c (or ax² + bx + c). 4. Four terms → grouping. Then check by multiplying out.
The classic mistakes: 6ab + 9ac − 3a = 3a(2b + 3c) (the 1 is lost), 4x² − 10x = 2(2x² − 5x) (not fully factorised), x² − 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) (signs: it's (x − 2)(x − 3)), and x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x − 3) (that gives x² − 6x + 9).

Expanding brackets came in Algebra 2. Solving quadratic equations by factorising comes in Quadratic equations; algebraic fractions and long division come in Algebra 4.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

"Factorise" (or "factorise fully") is one of the most common short parts on the paper, often with three parts: a common factor, grouping or a trinomial, and a difference of two squares. Factorising also turns up inside bigger questions: solving a quadratic equation, simplifying an algebraic fraction, finding the sides of a rectangle from its area, and working out sums like 87² − 13² without a calculator.

How marks are won and lost

  • Take out the highest common factor first, and check nothing is left to take out ("fully").
  • Show the middle line in grouping: x(y + 4) − 3(y + 4). It earns marks even if you slip after.
  • Watch the signs: for x² − x − 12 the numbers are 3 and −4, not −3 and 4.
  • Don't lose a 1 when a whole term comes out: 5x² + x = x(5x + 1).
  • Check by multiplying out, or put x = 1 into the question and your answer.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. Factorise. (a) 6x² − 15x  (b) xy + 4x − 3y − 12  (c) x² − 64
    Show the worked answer

    (a) HCF = 3x: 3x(2x − 5).
    (b) x(y + 4) − 3(y + 4) = (y + 4)(x − 3).
    (c) x² − 8² = (x − 8)(x + 8).

  2. The rectangle. A rectangle has area x² − 2x − 24 cm² and its length is x + 4 cm. (a) Factorise x² − 2x − 24. (b) Write the width of the rectangle. (c) Find the length and the width when x = 10, and check that they give the right area.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Multiply to −24, add to −2: 4 and −6. (x + 4)(x − 6).
    (b) Width = x − 6 cm.
    (c) Length 14 cm, width 4 cm: 14 × 4 = 56. And 10² − 2(10) − 24 = 100 − 20 − 24 = 56 cm² ✓.

  3. Squares. (a) Use factorising to work out 87² − 13² without a calculator. (b) Factorise fully 5x² − 45. (c) Higher Level: factorise 4x² − 9y².
    Show the worked answer

    (a) (87 − 13)(87 + 13) = 74 × 100 = 7,400.
    (b) 5(x² − 9) = 5(x − 3)(x + 3).
    (c) (2x)² − (3y)² = (2x − 3y)(2x + 3y).

  4. Spot the mistake. Ciara wrote 2x² + 8x = 2(x² + 4x). (a) Is her answer equal to 2x² + 8x? Is it fully factorised? (b) Write the fully factorised form. (c) Higher Level: factorise 2x² + 7x + 3 and check your answer with x = 1.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Yes, 2(x² + 4x) = 2x² + 8x, but it is not fully factorised: x² + 4x still has x in both terms.
    (b) 2x(x + 4).
    (c) (2x + 1)(x + 3). Check with x = 1: 2 + 7 + 3 = 12, and (3)(4) = 12 ✓.
