Short stories

A short story is small but it has a full shape: a setting, a problem, a moment where everything turns, and an ending.

Spot the pattern

📦 The Parcel

1A parcel with no name on it arrived at our house.

2It was heavy and it ticked quietly.

3Grandad had been talking about his broken alarm clock all week.

Ending A

It turned out a famous footballer had sent it by mistake.

Ending B

It turned out to be a new alarm clock that Grandad had ordered online.

Both endings surprise you. Which one feels clever, and which one feels like a cheat? Which line of the story does the better ending reach back to?

Show me

B is a real twist: the clue was planted in line 3 (the broken alarm clock), so the ending surprises you but makes sense when you look back. The ticking was a clock all along.
A comes from nowhere: nothing in the story set it up. “Then I woke up. It was all a dream.” is the same kind of cheat.

The shape of a story

🏠 SettingWhere and when: “It was a wet Saturday morning on Kilkee beach…”
⚔️ ProblemSomething goes wrong: lost, stuck, missing, broken.
💥 ClimaxThe big moment: “Suddenly…”, “Just then…”, “At that moment…”
🏁 EndingHow it works out: “In the end…”, “Finally…”, “From that day on…”

Your teacher may call these exposition, rising action (the problem builds), climax and resolution.

Openings and twists

💬 DialogueStart with someone speaking: “Don't open that door!”
🏃 ActionStart in the middle of something happening.
❓ MysteryStart with something strange the reader wants explained.
🎁 TwistA surprise ending that was set up by a clue earlier.
📍 Setting cluesWriters rarely say “at the beach”. They show it: sand, gulls, the tide.
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Short stories turn up in two ways. In reading questions you get an unseen extract and are asked about the setting, the characters, the tension or the ending, with evidence from the text. In writing you may be asked to write a short story, or the opening or ending of one.

How marks are won and lost

  • Quote the clue words when you talk about setting or mood: don't just say “it's scary”.
  • Name the part of the plot and say what it does: “This is the climax because…”.
  • When you write: one problem, a few characters, a strong first line and an ending you planned from the start.
  • Avoid “It was all a dream” and endings that come from nowhere.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

The Lighthouse Key
It was a wild October night on the Beara peninsula, and rain hammered the windows of Gran's cottage. Gran had gone to bed early, leaving Cormac alone with the crackling fire. At ten o'clock, the lighthouse on the point went dark. Cormac knew the fishing boats depended on that light, and the old keeper had been sick all week. Suddenly, he remembered the heavy iron key that always hung on the hook by Gran's door. He grabbed his torch and ran out into the storm.
  1. Where and when is the story set? Quote two words or phrases that help you picture it.
    Show a model answer

    It is set on a stormy October night in Gran's cottage on the Beara peninsula, near a lighthouse. “Rain hammered the windows” and “the crackling fire” help me picture the wild weather outside and the warm room inside.
    Where the marks would go: place and time both named, two short quotes, a word on what they help you picture.

  2. What is the problem in this extract, and which sentence is the turning point?
    Show a model answer

    The problem is that the lighthouse goes dark and the fishing boats depend on it, but the keeper is sick. The turning point is “Suddenly, he remembered the heavy iron key…”, because that is when Cormac decides to act.
    Where the marks would go: the problem in your own words, the turning point quoted, and a reason why it is the turning point.

  3. How does the writer build tension in the extract? Make two points.
    Show a model answer

    First, the storm and the dark: the rain “hammered” and the light “went dark”, so the night feels dangerous. Second, Cormac is “alone”, with Gran asleep and the keeper sick, so nobody else can help. The last sentence ends with him running “out into the storm”, so we want to know what happens next.
    Where the marks would go: two separate points, each with a short quote and a sentence explaining the effect on the reader.

  4. Write an ending for the story (4–6 sentences) with a twist that is set up by a clue in the extract.
    Show a model answer

    When Cormac reached the lighthouse, the heavy door was already open. Up the winding stairs, a figure in a dripping coat was winding the great lamp. Slowly, the beam swept out across the bay again. The figure turned around. It was Gran. It turned out that for forty years she had been the keeper's secret helper, and the key by her door had never been just for decoration.
    Where the marks would go: the ending follows on from the extract, the twist is set up by a clue that was already there (the key by Gran's door, Gran “gone to bed early”), and the sentences build to the reveal.
