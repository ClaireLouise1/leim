Exam technique

The Junior Cycle English exam is one paper, 2 hours, 180 marks, at Higher and at Ordinary level. Every question links to the paper's theme, and the sections and their marks change from year to year. So you can't learn the paper off. What you can learn is how to read a question and how to use your time.

Spot the pattern

A: Identify two facts about the festival.B: Explain how the writer builds tension.C: Compare the openings of the two stories.

All three are about a text. What is different about the answer each one wants? Which one needs the shortest answer?

Show me

The first word, the command word, tells you the job. A wants two short, exact details: no explaining. B wants how: name the method, quote it, say what it does. C wants both texts in every point: how they are alike and how they differ. A is the shortest. Answering B like A (just listing) loses most of the marks.

Command words

The question says…So you…
Identify, name, list, write down, pick out, find🔎 find it in the text: short and exact
Describe📝 say what it is like, with details
Explain, why, how💡 say why or how, and back it up
Compare, contrast⚖️ alike AND different, both texts every time
Do you agree, do you think, in your opinion🙋 give your view clearly, then reasons
Summarise, sum up✂️ main points only, short, your own words

Time it by the marks

⏱️ 40 seconds a mark120 minutes ÷ 180 marks = ⅔ of a minute a mark. A 30-mark question gets about 20 minutes, a 60-mark question about 40.
🛑 Move onWhen the time for a question is up, finish your sentence and go on. Marks for an answer you never started are easier to win than the last few marks of a long one.

Keep the last few minutes to read your answers back: missing words, spelling, full stops and capitals. In the exam itself, use the marks printed on your paper: they change from year to year.

Read the question twice

  • Underline the command word (explain, describe, compare…).
  • Circle how many: two reasons, three details, one quote.
  • Box the focus: which character, which paragraph, which feeling.
  • "With reference to the text" = quote or point to it: point, quote, explain.
  • For writing tasks: form (letter, speech, article…), reader (who is it for?) and purpose (to persuade, inform, entertain).
Exam corner

How the paper works

One paper, 2 hours, 180 marks. The paper has a theme (for example, recent Higher Level papers used "Interesting Interactions", "Perception" and "Critical Reflection"), and every text and task links to it. There is usually an unseen text to read and answer on, questions on the texts you studied (novel, drama, poetry, film, and Shakespeare at Higher Level), and writing tasks such as a story, a speech, a letter or an article. The sections and marks change, so read the whole paper first and plan your time.

How marks are won and lost

  • Answer the question that was asked, not the one you hoped for. Use the question's words in your first sentence.
  • Back up every point with a short quote or a clear reference.
  • Spend time in line with the marks. A long answer to a small question costs you marks somewhere else.
  • Leave nothing blank. A short answer with one good point beats an empty space.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. A section of a paper has three questions worth 15, 15 and 30 marks. How long should you spend on each? Why?
    Show a model answer

    About 10, 10 and 20 minutes. The paper is 120 minutes for 180 marks, so each mark gets ⅔ of a minute (40 seconds): 15 × ⅔ = 10 and 30 × ⅔ = 20. The 30-mark question is worth as much as the other two together, so it gets as much time as both.
    Where the marks would go: the three times; the reason (time follows the marks).

  2. Read this question: "Explain two ways the writer makes the opening of the story exciting. Support your answer with reference to the text." What exactly does it ask you to do?
    Show a model answer

    Command word: explain, so say how, not just what happens. How many: two ways, so two paragraphs. Focus: the opening only, and "exciting" (not sad, not funny). Reference: a short quote for each way. A plan: (1) short sentences build tension: quote + what it does; (2) a mystery the reader wants solved: quote + what it does.
    Where the marks would go: two clear ways; a quote for each; an explanation of why each is exciting.

  3. A student answered "How does Seán feel at the end of the extract?" with: "Seán goes home and talks to his mam. Then he goes to bed." Why would this lose marks? Rewrite it.
    Show a model answer

    It retells what happens; it never names a feeling or uses the text. Better: "At the end, Seán feels relieved. He 'let out a long breath' when his mam hugged him, which shows the worry he had carried all day is finally gone."
    Where the marks would go: a feeling named (point); a quote (evidence); what the quote shows (explain).

  4. Writing task: "Write the text of a talk for your class about a place that means a lot to you." Name the form, the reader and the purpose, and make a five-line plan.
    Show a model answer

    Form: a talk, so speak to the audience ("Have you ever…?", "Thank you for listening"). Reader: your class: friendly, but no slang. Purpose: to describe and to share why it matters. Plan: 1. Hook: a question about a favourite place. 2. Where it is and what you see, hear and smell. 3. A memory there. 4. Why it matters now. 5. Ending: invite them to find their own place; thank them.
    Where the marks would go: a clear talk shape (opening, paragraphs, ending); writing that suits the class; details and a personal voice.
