Shakespeare (Higher Level)

At Higher Level you study one full Shakespeare play. The plays on the Department's list for the 2028–2030 exams are As You Like It, Julius Caesar, The Merchant of Venice and Romeo and Juliet (check with your teacher which list your year uses). The game uses the last three. You don't need to learn the whole play off by heart: you need to know the story, the characters, a handful of key scenes and some short quotes.

Spot the pattern

Juliet: “Deny thy father and refuse thy name.”Tybalt: “Thou art a villain.”Tybalt: “I hate … all Montagues, and thee.”

Thou, thee and thy all mean one thing in modern English. What is it? When do you think each one is used?

Show me

They all mean you. They follow the same pattern as I / me / my: thou = you doing the action (like I), thee = you on the receiving end (like me), thy = your (like my). Shakespeare's characters use thou to friends, family and people below them, and the more formal “you” to people above them, so a sudden switch can show anger or affection. The verbs change too: thou art (are), thou dost (do), he doth (does), he hath (has).

How Shakespeare writes

Verselines with a beat (often 10 syllables, da-DUM × 5). Noble characters and big feelings.
Proseordinary sentences. Servants, jokes, and characters who've lost control.
Soliloquya character alone on stage speaks their thoughts: we hear the truth.
Asidea quick comment to the audience that the others on stage can't hear.
Dramatic ironywe know something a character doesn't (we know Juliet isn't really dead).

Techniques to spot

🔀 Oxymorontwo opposites side by side: “sweet sorrow”, “loving hate”.
🤝 Similea comparison with like or as: “as boundless as the sea”.
🎵 Rhyming couplettwo rhyming lines, often at the end of a scene.
❓ Rhetorical questionasked for effect: “Hath not a Jew eyes?”
🔁 Repetition“For Brutus is an honourable man” said again and again until it sounds like a lie.
😏 Puna play on words: the dying Mercutio's “a grave man”.

Key moments

📕 Romeo and JulietThe party → the balcony → the secret wedding → Mercutio and Tybalt die → Capulet's fury → the potion → the tomb.
💰 The Merchant of VeniceThe loan and the bond → Jessica runs away → Shylock's revenge → the caskets → the trial: mercy, then “no blood” → Belmont.
🗡️ Julius CaesarThe Soothsayer's warning → Cassius works on Brutus → the plot → Caesar is stabbed → Antony's speech → war → Brutus dies.

Answering on Shakespeare

Make a point, back it with a short quote (a few words is best), and explain what the words show. Name the scene (“in Act 3, Scene 1”), say how the audience feels, and mention staging when it helps: what would we see and hear on stage? Learn 3–4 short quotes for each main character and for each big theme (love and hate, fate, revenge, mercy, ambition, loyalty).

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Higher Level only. The paper can give you an extract from a Shakespeare play (sometimes one you haven't studied) and ask about character, language and staging, and it can ask about the Shakespeare play you studied: a key scene, a character, a theme, or how you would stage a moment. Marks go to short, accurate quotes, a clear point each time and an explanation of how the language or staging works, not to retelling the plot.

How marks are won and lost

  • Quote briefly and accurately: a few words, in quotation marks, spelt right.
  • Explain every quote: what do the words show? Why that word?
  • Know where it happens: the act, the scene, what has just happened.
  • Think like a director: how would you stage it? Lighting, movement, tone of voice.
  • Don't retell the story. One sentence of plot is enough.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). The extract is from Romeo and Juliet, Act 3, Scene 1. "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

Tybalt has just stabbed Mercutio under Romeo's arm, as Romeo tried to stop the fight. Mercutio: I am hurt. A plague o' both your houses! I am sped. Benvolio: What, art thou hurt? Mercutio: Ay, ay, a scratch, a scratch; marry, 'tis enough. Where is my page? Go, villain, fetch a surgeon. Romeo: Courage, man; the hurt cannot be much. Mercutio: No, 'tis not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church-door; but 'tis enough, 'twill serve: ask for me to-morrow, and you shall find me a grave man. … Why the devil came you between us? I was hurt under your arm. Romeo: I thought all for the best.
  1. Mercutio calls his wound “a scratch, a scratch”. What does this show about him?
    Show a model answer

    It shows Mercutio is brave and proud, and still joking: he plays down a deadly wound as “a scratch”. But “'tis enough” and “fetch a surgeon” tell us he knows it is serious. The audience realises before Romeo does that he is dying (dramatic irony), so the joke makes the moment sadder.
    Where the marks would go: a clear quality; the quote; the gap between what he says and what he knows; the effect on the audience.

  2. Explain the pun in “you shall find me a grave man”. Why do you think Shakespeare gives Mercutio a joke here?
    Show a model answer

    “Grave” means serious and also a grave to be buried in: tomorrow he'll be a serious man because he'll be dead. Mercutio has joked all through the play, so he dies as he lived, with wordplay. The joke also shows the turn in the play: the comedy stops here and the tragedy begins.
    Where the marks would go: both meanings; what it shows about Mercutio; why it matters at this point in the play.

  3. “A plague o' both your houses!” Why is this line so important to the play as a whole?
    Show a model answer

    Mercutio is neither a Capulet nor a Montague, yet the feud has killed him, so he curses both families. The curse seems to come true: from this moment everything goes wrong (Tybalt's death, Romeo's banishment, the deaths in the tomb). He repeats it three times, so the audience can't miss it.
    Where the marks would go: who he blames and why; the link to later events (fate, the feud); the effect of the repetition.

  4. Choose a key scene from the Shakespeare play you studied. Explain why it is a turning point, and how you would stage one moment from it.
    Show a model answer

    Plan for any play: (1) Name the play, the act and scene, and what happens, in two sentences. (2) Why it is a turning point: what changes for the characters afterwards? (3) Two short quotes, each explained. (4) Staging one moment: lighting, sound, where the actors stand, a tone of voice, and the effect on the audience. (5) One line linking the scene to a big theme (love and hate, fate, mercy, ambition).
    Where the marks would go: a specific scene; a clear reason it is a turning point; accurate short quotes explained; detailed staging ideas with their effect; no long plot summary.
