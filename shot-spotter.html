Film: camera shots, sound & key scenes

A film is a story told in pictures and sound. Every shot is a choice the director made: how close the camera is, where it is, what we hear and how the shots are joined. In the exam you write about what the director chose and why.

Spot the pattern

The same girl, three frames.

What changes from A to C? What can you see in C that you couldn't see in A? What can you see in A that you lose in C?

Show me

The camera moves closer. A is a long shot: the whole body, so we see what she is doing and where. B is a medium shot, waist up. C is a close-up: her face fills the screen, so we see her feelings, but we lose the place. The closer the camera, the more we feel; the further away, the more we see of the setting.

Shot sizes: how close is the camera?

🏔️ Extreme long shotthe person is tiny; the place fills the screen. Often the establishing shot that shows where we are.
🧍 Long shotthe whole body, head to feet: what someone is doing.
👕 Medium shotwaist up: two people talking.
🙂 Close-upface and shoulders: feelings.
👁️ Extreme close-upjust the eyes, a hand, a key: a detail or a strong feeling.

Camera angles: where is the camera?

⬆️ Low anglelooks up: the character seems big, powerful, frightening.
⬇️ High anglelooks down: the character seems small, weak, alone.
👀 Eye levellevel with the eyes: normal, like meeting them.
🦅 Bird's-eye viewright above, looking straight down.
👁️ Point-of-view shotwe see through a character's eyes.

Sound and music

🎬 Diegetic soundcomes from inside the story: dialogue, footsteps, a radio. The characters can hear it.
🎧 Non-diegetic soundis added for us: the soundtrack music, a voice-over. The characters can't hear it.
🎵 Musictells us how to feel: low, fast drums = tension; slow piano = sadness; bright fiddle = joy.
🤫 Silencecan be the loudest choice: it makes a moment stand out.

Editing: how the shots are joined

⚡ Quick cutsmany short shots: speed, panic, tension.
🐢 Slow motiona big moment lasts longer.
⏪ Flashbacka jump back to the past.
🌑 Fade to blackends a scene, or shows time passing.
🎞️ Montageshort clips that squeeze days or weeks into a minute.

Writing about a key scene

Pick one key scene (a turning point, the climax, the ending). Say what happens in a sentence, then name two or three techniques and explain each one: Name it (a low-angle shot), describe it (we look up at the coach on the touchline), say what it does (he seems powerful and frightening, so we fear for the team). Use the director's name and the film's title.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Every student studies a film (from the Department's list), and the paper can ask about it: a key scene, a character, how the director creates tension or mood, or a film compared with another text. You may also get an unseen still or a film poster to write about. Marks go to named techniques, specific moments from your film and an explanation of the effect on the audience, not to a retelling of the plot.

How marks are won and lost

  • Name the technique (close-up, low angle, diegetic sound), don't just say “the camera”.
  • Be specific: which scene, which moment, which character.
  • Explain the effect: what does it make us feel or think?
  • Don't retell the whole story. One sentence of plot is enough.
  • Learn the director's name and the year, and spell them right.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "The Crossing" is an imaginary film. "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

A key scene from The Crossing
Orla (14) must row her little brother Cian across to the island before the storm.
Shot 1: an extreme long shot: a tiny boat on a grey sea, the island far away. Wind howls.
Shot 2: a close-up of Orla's hands gripping the oars, her knuckles white.
Shot 3: a low-angle shot of Cian standing in the bow, pointing at the island.
Shot 4: a point-of-view shot: the harbour wall coming closer through the spray.
Sound: low drums on the soundtrack get faster and faster; then silence as the boat bumps the pier, and a gull cries.
  1. Why do you think the director opens the scene with an extreme long shot?
    Show a model answer

    The extreme long shot makes the boat look tiny against the huge grey sea, with the island “far away”. It shows us at once how dangerous the crossing is and how small and alone the children are, so we are worried for them before we even see their faces.
    Where the marks would go: the shot named; what we see; the effect on the audience (danger, worry).

  2. What does Shot 2 tell us about Orla? Explain how.
    Show a model answer

    The close-up of her hands “gripping the oars” with “knuckles white” shows she is terrified but determined. We don't need to see her face: the close-up on a small detail shows how hard she is fighting the sea and how much she wants to get Cian across.
    Where the marks would go: a quality or feeling; the detail quoted; how the close-up shows it.

  3. Name one diegetic and one non-diegetic sound in the scene. Why is the silence at the end effective?
    Show a model answer

    The howling wind and the gull are diegetic (Orla can hear them); the drums are non-diegetic (added on the soundtrack). The drums get faster to build tension, so when they suddenly stop as the boat bumps the pier, the silence feels like a held breath let out: the danger is over. The single gull cry tells us they are safe on land.
    Where the marks would go: one correct example of each; the build-up of the drums; the effect of the silence.

  4. Choose a key scene from a film you have studied. Explain how the director uses camera shots and sound to create tension.
    Show a model answer

    Plan for any film: (1) Name the film, the director and the scene in one sentence. (2) A camera shot: name it, describe the moment, say what it makes us feel (“the low-angle shot of the coach makes him seem…”). (3) A second shot or angle, the same way. (4) Sound: music, diegetic sound or silence, and how it builds or breaks the tension. (5) One line on why this scene matters to the whole film.
    Where the marks would go: a specific scene; two or three techniques correctly named; clear explanation of the effect each time; good use of film words; no long plot summary.
