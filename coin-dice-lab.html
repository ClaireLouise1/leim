Probability 2: relative frequency & counting

Spot the pattern: the lab

Throw a fair coin 10 times and work out the relative frequency of heads (heads ÷ throws). Then keep going to 100, 1,000 and 10,000 throws.

  • After 10 throws, is the relative frequency exactly 0.5? Press "Start again" and try 10 more. Do you get the same?
  • What happens to the relative frequency as the number of throws gets bigger?
Show me

After a few throws the relative frequency jumps around (0.3, 0.7…) and changes every time you start again. After thousands of throws it settles down close to the probability, 0.5 for heads (or 1/6 ≈ 0.167 for a six). So: the more trials, the better the relative frequency is as an estimate of the probability. That's how we find probabilities we can't work out by counting, like a drawing pin landing point up.

Relative frequency

Relative frequency = number of times it happened ÷ number of trials

A drawing pin is dropped 50 times and lands point up 18 times. Relative frequency = 18 ÷ 50 = 0.36 (or 36%, or 9/25). It is always between 0 and 1.

Estimate a probabilityWhen outcomes are NOT equally likely (a drawing pin, a bent coin, a bottle flip, a penalty), do an experiment. The relative frequency is your estimate of the probability.
More trials = betterAn estimate from 500 trials is more reliable than one from 20. Two people doing the same experiment usually get different results, and that's normal.
Putting results togetherCiara: 12 out of 30. Seán: 18 out of 50. Together: 30 out of 80 = 0.375. Add the counts first; don't take the mean of 0.4 and 0.36.

Expected frequency

Expected frequency = probability × number of trials
From a fair modelA fair die is rolled 300 times. Expected sixes = 1/6 × 300 = 50. You'll get about 50, not exactly 50.
From an experiment18 of 50 seeds grew (0.36). Plant 1,000: expect 0.36 × 1,000 = 360 to grow.
Working backwardsP(red) = 1/4. To expect 30 reds: trials × 1/4 = 30, so trials = 30 × 4 = 120.
What's in the bag?20 counters; picked and put back 200 times; red 50 times. 50 ÷ 200 = 0.25, so about 0.25 × 20 = 5 red counters.

Fair or biased?

A die is fair if every score is equally likely. Roll it many times and compare the results with what you'd expect. A die rolled 600 times should give about 100 of each score.

The fundamental principle of counting

If one thing can be done in m ways and another in n ways, both together can be done in m × n ways
Menus and outfits3 starters, 4 mains, 2 desserts: 3 × 4 × 2 = 24 lunch deals. A tree diagram would have 24 branch ends, a two-way table 3 × 4 cells for the first two choices.
Codes (repeats allowed)A lock with 3 dials, each 0 to 9: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 codes. One letter A–Z then one digit: 26 × 10 = 260.
No repeats: the choices go down4 friends in a row: 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. Gold, silver and bronze from 8 runners: 8 × 7 × 6 = 336.
Counting and probabilityA 3-dial code set at random: P(it's 7 1 3) = 1/1,000. Count all the outcomes, then the ones you want.

Classic mistakes

  • Dividing by "the rest" (18 ÷ 32) instead of by the number of trials (18 ÷ 50).
  • Thinking a fair die rolled 60 times gives exactly 10 sixes, or that a coin "is due" a tail after 5 heads (it has no memory).
  • Trusting a result from 10 trials more than one from 1,000.
  • Adding the choices (3 + 4 + 2) instead of multiplying them (3 × 4 × 2).
  • Forgetting a 2-way choice like "with or without a name" (it doubles the total).
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Relative frequency and counting usually come as one part of a bigger probability question, on both levels: a table of experiment results to turn into relative frequencies, a "how many would you expect?" prediction, a "is it fair?" judgement with a reason, or a counting question about clothes, menus, codes or teams. The reason ("more trials give a better estimate") is often worth as much as the number.

How marks are won and lost

  • Show the division. "18 ÷ 50 = 0.36" earns marks even if you then slip.
  • Write it the way you're asked: decimal, percentage or fraction.
  • Give a reason with numbers. "Biased, because a 6 came up 205 times but we'd expect about 100" beats "biased".
  • Multiply, don't add, for counting, and show the multiplication.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. Bottle flip. Ciara flips a water bottle 80 times and it lands standing up 12 times. (a) Find the relative frequency of the bottle landing standing up. (b) Ciara is going to flip the bottle 500 times. How many times would you expect it to land standing up? (c) Seán flipped the same bottle 20 times and it landed standing up 6 times. Whose results give the better estimate of the probability? Give a reason. (d) They put their results together. Find the new relative frequency.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15.
    (b) 0.15 × 500 = 75 times.
    (c) Ciara's: she did more trials (80 v 20), and more trials give a better estimate.
    (d) 12 + 6 = 18 standing up out of 80 + 20 = 100 flips: 18 ÷ 100 = 0.18.

  2. Is the spinner fair? A spinner with 4 equal sectors is spun 200 times.
    ColourFrequency
    Red52
    Blue47
    Green49
    Yellow52
    Total200
    (a) If the spinner is fair, how many times would you expect each colour? (b) Find the relative frequency of each colour. (c) Do you think the spinner is fair? Give a reason. (d) Use the results to estimate how many times it would land on red in 1,000 spins.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 1/4 × 200 = 50 each.
    (b) Red 52 ÷ 200 = 0.26, blue 0.235, green 0.245, yellow 0.26.
    (c) Yes, probably fair: every result is close to 50 (every relative frequency is close to 0.25). Results are never exactly equal.
    (d) 0.26 × 1,000 = 260.

  3. School hoodies. A school hoodie comes in 5 colours (one of them navy) and 4 sizes, with or without a name printed on the back. (a) How many different hoodies are possible? (b) The school adds 2 more colours. How many different hoodies are possible now? (c) Before the new colours, one of the hoodie types from (a) is picked at random. What is the probability that it is navy?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 5 × 4 × 2 = 40.
    (b) 7 × 4 × 2 = 56.
    (c) Navy hoodies: 1 × 4 × 2 = 8 of the 40, so P = 8/40 = 1/5.

  4. A bike lock. A bike lock has 3 dials, each numbered 0 to 9. (a) How many different codes are possible? (b) Aoife forgets her code but knows it starts with 7. How many codes might she have to try? (c) Each try takes 4 seconds. What is the longest it could take her? Give your answer in minutes and seconds. (d) How many codes have three different digits?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.
    (b) 1 × 10 × 10 = 100.
    (c) 100 × 4 = 400 seconds = 6 minutes 40 seconds.
    (d) 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 (the second dial can't repeat the first, and the third can't repeat either).
