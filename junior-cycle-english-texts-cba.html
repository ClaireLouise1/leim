The Collection of Texts: a guide for students

How to build your collection of writing over 2nd and 3rd year, the genres to choose from, how to redraft, how the two pieces you pick are judged, and the reflection note.

What it isGenresBuild itRedraftingPick your twoHow it's judgedReflection noteExamplePractise

What it is

  • Over 2nd and 3rd year you keep a collection of your own writing: at least four texts, in at least four different genres.
  • Most pieces come from normal class work: write a report in the report-writing unit, and it can go in. A stronger piece can replace a weaker one later.
  • Your drafts stay in the collection too. They show how the piece grew, but they aren't graded.
  • A "text" can be handwritten, typed, digital or multimodal (words with images, audio or video).
  • In 3rd year you choose two texts to be assessed (your best, or with your teacher's advice), and write a Student Reflection Note for each.
This year's rule (exams in 2027 and 2028): every student does at least one English CBA, and the school decides whether it's one or both. Ask your teacher which one your class is doing, and when. The written Assessment Task is not examined, so the June exam is 100% of the English grade. Dates and rules for this school year →

Keep everything: plans, first drafts, your teacher's comments. You'll need them for your reflection notes, and they show how much you improved.

Redrafting: where the improvement happens

  1. PlanWho's it for, and what should they think or feel at the end? Note your ideas, then put them in order.
  2. DraftGet it all down. Don't fix as you go.
  3. Get feedbackFrom your teacher or a classmate: one thing that works, one thing to improve.
  4. RedraftChange the big things first: the opening, the order, a weak ending, a dull paragraph. Swap "telling" for "showing".
  5. Edit and proofreadThen the small things: spelling, punctuation, repeated words, paragraphing. Read it out loud: you'll hear the mistakes.

A redraft in action

First draft

The bus was cold and late. There was a man sleeping and two girls listening to music. It smelled bad. Then we got to the town.

Redraft

The 10.40 was always late, and always cold. A man in a high-vis jacket slept with his forehead on the glass, while two girls shared one set of earphones and laughed at something only they could hear.

The redraft swaps a list of facts for specific details the reader can see. That's the kind of change that moves a piece up a level. (The full piece is on the CBA guide for parents.)

Pick your two

  • Pick your two strongest, and ask your teacher if you're unsure.
  • Both have to be strong. If your two pieces get different levels, the lower one is reported. One brilliant piece and one rushed piece gives you the rushed piece's level.
  • Show range. Two different genres (say, a story and an opinion piece) show more of what you can do than two of the same kind.
  • Pick pieces you can write about. You'll need to explain why you chose the genre and what you learned, so choose ones with a story behind them.

How it's judged

Each of your two texts is judged on how well you handle its genre, the quality of the writing and word choice, and how well it's shaped for its audience. The four levels:

LevelWhat it looks like
ExceptionalCreative and in full command of the genre; highly skilled writing with original ideas and imaginative word choices that fit the purpose perfectly; fully shaped for its reader.
Above ExpectationsVery good control of the genre; consistently skilled writing with effective word choices for the purpose; clearly written with the reader in mind.
In Line with ExpectationsGood awareness of the genre; generally skilled writing with word choices that suit the purpose; the reader is kept in mind throughout.
Yet to Meet ExpectationsLittle awareness of the genre; the writing isn't yet skilled and word choices may not suit the purpose; little sense of who it's for.

Your teacher reads the whole piece and decides which level fits best, starting at the lowest and moving up (an "on balance" judgement), so one weak moment doesn't sink it. The level words are the NCCA's; the descriptions are ours, in plain English.

The Student Reflection Note (one per text)

  1. The title and the genre
  2. Why you chose that genre, briefly
  3. A reflection on the piece: what you were trying to do, what you learned writing it, and what you'd do differently next time

Your drafts help here: look at what changed between them.

Example: a critique, with its reflection note

Written by Léim, as an example of the length and style of a strong piece. (The school show in it is made up.)

Review: "The Wizard of Oz", our school musical

Three nights, one tornado and 140 nervous students: our school's "Wizard of Oz" was ambitious, and for most of the night it worked.

The star was the set. The art department turned the hall into Kansas with grey sheets and a wind machine, then pulled them down in seconds to reveal Oz in every colour they could find. The whole audience gasped, and so did I.

Aoife Byrne, as Dorothy, carried the show. Her "Over the Rainbow" was quiet and simple, and the hall went completely still. The Tin Man got the biggest laughs, mostly because his costume squeaked whenever he moved.

It wasn't perfect. The second half dragged, and the flying monkeys were hard to hear over the band. Twenty minutes shorter would have been ten times better.

Still, it's the best thing our school has put on in years. Four stars out of five. Bring tissues for the ending, and earplugs for the monkeys.

Its reflection note

Title and genre: Review: "The Wizard of Oz", our school musical. Critique (a review).

Why this genre: I'd only ever written stories, and I wanted to try writing an opinion that's fair: praising what worked and being honest about what didn't.

What I was trying to do: make readers who missed the show feel they'd seen it, and help them judge it for themselves.

What I learned: my first draft just retold the story of the musical. In my redraft I cut the plot to one line and gave every paragraph one judgement with an example (the set, Dorothy, the problems).

Next time: I'd interview someone in the cast, to add a quote and another point of view.

Why the review works: it opens with a hook and an overall verdict, every paragraph makes one judgement backed by a specific detail, it's honest about the weak points, and the ending gives a clear rating and a joke that sounds like a real reviewer.
