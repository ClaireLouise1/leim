Oral Communication: a step-by-step guide

Everything you need for the English oral CBA: what it is, picking a topic, a 3-week plan, how to shape a talk that holds the room, how it's judged, and the reflection note.

What it is

  • About 3 minutes, and that includes the time you spend engaging your audience (questions, a show of hands). Your teacher may ask you questions too.
  • Alone or in a group. In a group, everyone needs a real part, and you're judged on your part.
  • Usually near the end of 2nd year, with about 3 school weeks to prepare it in class.
  • Any format that suits you: a presentation (notes are fine, and so is reading from a script you wrote), a performance (a scene, a sketch, a poem), an interview (being interviewed, or doing the interviewing) or a response to something (a picture, a song, a poem, a clip). A debate, a radio show, a sports commentary or a mock interview all count.
  • Afterwards you write a short Student Reflection Note (see below).
This year's rule (exams in 2027 and 2028): every student does at least one English CBA, and the school decides whether it's one or both. Ask your teacher which one your class is doing, and when. The written Assessment Task is not examined, so the June exam is 100% of the English grade. Dates and rules for this school year →

Pick a topic

Choose something you actually care about. It doesn't have to come from English class. Then turn it into one key question: it keeps your research and your talk on track.

InterestKey question
SportWhy do so many teenage girls give up sport, and what would keep them playing?
Your familyHow was my grandmother's school different from mine? (an interview)
GamesCan a video game tell a story as well as a novel?
Your townWhat was our main street like 100 years ago?
MusicWhy do some songs get stuck in your head?
School lifeShould our school start at 9.30?
A book or filmIs the villain in my favourite book actually the hero?
A skillCan anyone learn to draw? (with a live demonstration)
A local businessHow did the café on our square start? (interview the owner)
HealthWhat really happens to your brain when you don't sleep?
Check before you commit: can you find at least two sources for it, and can you get it ready in three weeks? A topic that needs you to travel or wait for replies is risky.

Your 3-week plan

  1. Week 1: ResearchWrite your key question at the top of a page. Use more than one source so you can check one against another, and note where each fact came from. Original research is great: a class survey, an interview, an email to an expert. Note it all; you'll need it for your reflection note.
  2. Week 2: Shape itPick your best three points and drop the rest. Write a hook, an ending and the bridges between points (see Shape it). Make cue cards: one card per part, a few words each, big writing. Plan how you'll involve the audience, and what you'd show (a picture, an object, one slide).
  3. Week 3: PractiseSay it out loud, timed, at least five times. Record yourself on a school device or practise on a friend or family member, and ask them one thing to fix. Practise answering two or three questions people might ask you.

Shape it: a talk that holds the room

Start strong (first 20 seconds)

  • A question to the audience: "Hands up if…"
  • A surprising fact or number from your research
  • A 3-line story: "Last summer, my grandad handed me a photo…"
  • An object: hold it up and say nothing for two seconds

End strong (last 20 seconds)

  • Go back to your hook ("So, hands up now if…")
  • Give the audience a challenge or one thing to remember
  • Answer your key question in one sentence
  • Then: "Thank you. Does anyone have a question?"

In the middle

  • Three points, signposted: "First…", "The second thing surprised me…", "Finally…". Listeners can't flick back a page, so tell them where you are.
  • One example or story per point. People remember stories, not lists.
  • Talk to the audience, not your cards: "you", "we", eye contact, pauses after a big fact.
  • If you're persuading, answer the other side: "Some people say… but…".

Example: cue cards for a 3-minute talk

Key question: "How was my grandmother's school different from mine?" A presentation built on an interview with her. (An example written by Léim. For a full example talk, see the CBA guide for parents.)

0:00–0:251 Hook Hold up her 1968 school photo. "Find the girl who looks like me." (pause) "That's my nan, aged 12."
0:25–1:002 Getting there Walked 3 miles · no school bus · "wet socks all day" · Show of hands: who walks to school?
1:00–1:403 The classroom 42 in one room · tables chanted out loud · ink pens, no calculators · I have a laptop and 26 classmates
1:40–2:204 What surprised me She loved it! · teacher read stories aloud on Fridays · "we had less, but we listened more"
2:20–3:005 Answer + end Different tools, same worries: friends, tests, being picked for teams · Back to photo: "Same face. Different world." · Questions?

Why it works: it opens with an object and a question, every card is a few words (so she talks, not reads), it uses original research (the interview), it compares then and now point by point, and it ends by answering the key question and going back to the hook.

How it's judged

Your teacher looks at three things: how well you know and control your material, how clearly it's shaped for a purpose, and how well you engage your audience. The four levels:

LevelWhat it looks like
ExceptionalFluent and fully in control of the material; shaped imaginatively for a very clear purpose; the audience is hooked the whole way.
Above ExpectationsClear and convincing, with very well-chosen material; fully shaped for its purpose; very effective with the audience.
In Line with ExpectationsClear and convincing most of the time, showing you know your topic; shaped for a purpose; keeps the audience with you reasonably well.
Yet to Meet ExpectationsShows some knowledge but isn't convincing; the purpose is often unclear; the audience is engaged only now and then.

Your teacher reads the whole piece and decides which level fits best, starting at the lowest and moving up (an "on balance" judgement), so one weak moment doesn't sink it. The level words are the NCCA's; the descriptions are ours, in plain English.

What this means for you: a talk with a clear purpose that keeps the audience involved scores better than a talk crammed with facts. Know your material well enough to look up from your cards.

The Student Reflection Note

Everyone writes their own, even in a group, and hands it to the teacher. It has three parts:

  1. A title
  2. Your role and your sources: what you did (and, in a group, which part was yours), and the materials or sources you used
  3. A short reflection: one important thing you learned, and what you'd change or improve next time

Example (for the cue cards above)

Title: Then and now: my nan's school and mine

My role and sources: I did this on my own. I interviewed my nan for 40 minutes and recorded it (with her permission), used her school photo and copybook, and checked the facts about school transport in the 1960s on a local history website and in a book from the library.

What I learned: a real person's story holds an audience better than facts. Everyone leaned in when I held up the photo.

Next time: I'd practise in front of people sooner. My first try at home was over 4 minutes, so I cut a whole card about uniforms.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a wall of text with your head down. Reading your own script is allowed, but look up, slow down and talk to people.
  • Too much information. Three points told well beat ten facts rushed.
  • Forgetting it's for an audience: no hook, no questions, no "you".
  • Copying from a website. Use sources, but put it in your own words and say where it came from.
  • Not timing it. Most first tries run long. Time it, then cut.
  • A group where one person does everything. Each person is judged on their own part.
