The paper explained

Junior Cycle Maths is one written paper in June of 3rd year, at Higher or Ordinary level. In 2026 both levels looked the same from the outside:

  • 2 hours and 270 marks
  • 13 questions, and every question must be answered: there is no choice, so every topic on the course matters
  • You write your answers in the exam booklet, and you get a Formulae and Tables booklet in the exam

The difference is in the questions: Higher level asks harder questions and covers some extra topics.

How it's marked

Maths is not marked one mark per step. The examiner reads your answer to a part and decides which level it reaches. Each level is worth a fixed number of marks.

The levels

  • No credit: nothing of real value.
  • Low partial credit: some useful work, for example a correct first step or the right formula.
  • Mid partial credit (D scales only): about half right.
  • High partial credit: almost there.
  • Full credit: correct.

A B scale has 3 levels (no credit, partial, full), a C scale has 4 and a D scale has 5.

The green box is full marks. The examiner can only give a mark on the scale, or one mark below full (see below), so there are no half marks.

One line of work is worth marks. Even low partial credit is 2 marks on a 5-mark part, 3 or 4 marks on a 10-mark part and 4 marks on a 15-mark part. A blank is always 0.

If you go wrong

A small slip costs 1 mark at most

The marking scheme calls these slips:

  • rounding wrongly, or not rounding when asked
  • leaving out the units (cm, €, m²…), when the question says units are needed
  • a small arithmetic slip that doesn't make the question easier
  • misreading a number, as long as it doesn't make the question easier

If everything else is right, one slip gives you full marks minus 1 (for example 9 out of 10). If your answer wasn't getting full marks anyway, one slip costs nothing. But two or more slips in the same part count as an error.

An error drops you one level

A real mistake (an error) usually drops your answer one level on the scale, and two errors drop it two levels. But if you've shown enough good work, you still keep at least low partial credit. Some parts are marked in steps instead: then a mistake in one step doesn't stop you getting credit for the other steps.

A wrong answer in part (a) doesn't ruin part (b)

If you got (a) wrong, use your wrong answer in (b) and carry on. Examiners accept work carried forward from an earlier part, as long as your mistake hasn't made the later part easier. You can still get full marks for (b).

Crossed it out? Had two goes?

  • For a worked answer (a calculation or a method), the examiner marks every attempt, even crossed-out ones, and gives you the best one. So don't scribble out work: put a single line through it and keep going.
  • For a "choose" answer (pick an option, tick a box, name one thing), if you leave two different answers, you get the mark for the worse one. Cross out the one you don't want.
  • Extra work after the answer generally isn't penalised, except when you're asked to explain or justify in words: then your whole answer counts, so don't add things that contradict it.

Always show your work

A right answer with no work can still lose marks: in recent marking schemes, an answer without enough supporting work usually gets only the lowest mark above zero, unless that part says otherwise. Show every step.

Ran out of space?

Write all your answers in the exam booklet, inside the answer areas. There's extra space at the back: if you use it, label it with the question number and part (for example "Q7(b)"). Work written outside the answer areas might not be seen.

Exam strategies

  1. Check the time on each question. Each question shows a suggested maximum time. If you go over, move on and come back at the end.
  2. Collect the easy marks first. Every question is compulsory, but you can do them in any order. Do your strong ones first to build up marks and confidence.
  3. Read the last line twice. "Give your answer correct to 2 decimal places", "in cm²", "as a fraction": these are the instructions that cost a mark when missed.
  4. Round only at the end. Keep full calculator values until the final answer.
  5. Check your calculator is in degrees before any trigonometry question.
  6. Use the Formulae and Tables booklet. You get one in the exam (you can't bring your own), so practise with it all year.
  7. Never leave a part blank. Write the formula, put in the numbers, draw a labelled diagram: one line can be worth marks.

Model answers

Four exam-style questions, one from each part of the course, written by Léim. Try each one first, then open the model answer to see the working and where the marks would go.

Number

A bill with VAT

A phone repair costs €84.60 before VAT. VAT at 23% is added. Find the total cost.

Show the model answer
  1. VAT = 84.60 × 0.23 = 19.458 = €19.46
  2. Total = 84.60 + 19.46 = €104.06
  3. Check: 84.60 × 1.23 = 104.058 ≈ €104.06 ✓

Where the marks would go

  • Low partial 23% written as 0.23 (or 1% = €0.846), or any correct start
  • High partial VAT of €19.46 found, but not added on
  • Full €104.06
  • −1 (slip) €104.058 not rounded to the cent, or no € sign

Tip: Multiplying by 1.23 does it in one step, but writing the two steps keeps the partial credit safe if you slip.

Algebra & functions

Tickets

An adult ticket costs €x. A child ticket costs €4 less. Two adult tickets and three child tickets cost €58. Write an equation and find the price of each ticket.

Show the model answer
  1. Child ticket = x − 4
  2. 2x + 3(x − 4) = 58
  3. 2x + 3x − 12 = 58
  4. 5x = 70, so x = 14
  5. Adult €14, child €10. Check: 2(14) + 3(10) = 28 + 30 = 58 ✓

Where the marks would go

  • Low partial x − 4 for the child ticket, or 2x for two adults
  • Mid partial The correct equation 2x + 3(x − 4) = 58
  • High partial x = 14, but the child price not given
  • Full Both prices, €14 and €10

Tip: Always answer the question that was asked. Here it asks for each ticket, so x = 14 on its own isn't the end.

Geometry & trigonometry

The ladder

A ladder 5 m long leans against a wall. Its foot is 1.4 m from the wall, on level ground.
(a) How high up the wall does it reach?
(b) Find the angle the ladder makes with the ground, to the nearest degree.

Show the model answer
  1. (a) Pythagoras: h² + 1.4² = 5²
  2. h² = 25 − 1.96 = 23.04
  3. h = √23.04 = 4.8 m
  4. (b) cos A = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse = 1.4 ÷ 5 = 0.28
  5. A = cos⁻¹(0.28) = 73.7…° = 74°

Where the marks would go

  • (a) Low partial Pythagoras written with the right numbers in
  • (a) Full 4.8 m
  • (b) Low partial A correct ratio, e.g. cos A = 1.4/5
  • (b) Full 74°
  • −1 (slip) 73.7° not rounded, or a missing unit

Tip: Draw the triangle and label it first: the ladder is always the hypotenuse. Check your calculator is in degrees.

Statistics & probability

Working backwards from a mean

Seán's mean mark in 5 tests is 62. After a sixth test his mean is 65. What did he get in the sixth test?

Show the model answer
  1. Total of 5 tests = 5 × 62 = 310
  2. Total of 6 tests = 6 × 65 = 390
  3. Sixth test = 390 − 310 = 80

Where the marks would go

  • Low partial One total found (310 or 390)
  • High partial Both totals found
  • Full 80

Tip: Mean questions that give you the mean and ask for a missing value are always done with totals.

CBAs (Classroom-Based Assessments)

The CBA is done in class, not in June, and it doesn't change the exam grade. In Maths it's a Mathematical Investigation or a Statistical Investigation: ask a question, collect or make the data, work it out, and say what you found.

  • CBAs explained, with example investigations
  • For a Statistical Investigation, practise the steps with Data Detective (good questions, data and tables) and Average Ace (graphs and averages).

Grades

Your marks out of 270 become a percentage, and the percentage gives the grade. These are the new grade bands used since the 2025 exams, the same at both levels. The marks below are rounded.

For parents

  • The June exam gives the grade. It's set and marked by the State Examinations Commission, at Higher or Ordinary level.
  • The CBA is separate. Students do at least one Classroom-Based Assessment in class (most schools do one) (a Mathematical or a Statistical Investigation). It appears on the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement, not in the exam grade. See CBAs explained, with example investigations.
  • Ask to see the working. When your child shows you homework, look for the steps, not just the answer. That habit is worth marks in June.
  • Try one question against the clock. Past papers and marking schemes are free on the SEC's exam archive ↗. Let your child do one question in its suggested time, then check it against the marking scheme together.
