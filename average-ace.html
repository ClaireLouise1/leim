Statistics: graphs & averages

Spot the pattern

Four friends build towers of cubes: 2, 7, 3 and 4 cubes. They want the towers to be fair, so they move cubes from the tall towers to the short ones until every tower is the same height.

2
7
3
4

How tall is each tower at the end? Now try 5, 1, 6 and 4. Can you find a quick way to get the answer without moving any cubes?

Show me

Every tower ends up 4 cubes tall (and 4 again for 5, 1, 6, 4). Moving cubes never changes the total (16), so share the total equally: 16 ÷ 4 towers = 4. That is the mean: total ÷ how many. It is the "fair share" value.

The three averages and the range

MeanAdd them all up, divide by how many. 3, 5, 8, 8 → 24 ÷ 4 = 6.
MedianPut them in order, take the middle one. Two in the middle? Halfway between them. 3, 5, 8, 8 → (5 + 8) ÷ 2 = 6.5.
ModeThe value that comes up most often. 3, 5, 8, 8 → 8. (It works for words too: the most popular answer.)
RangeBiggest − smallest. 3, 5, 8, 8 → 8 − 3 = 5. It measures spread, not a typical value.
  • Missing value: the mean of 5 numbers is 7, so they add up to 5 × 7 = 35. Subtract the ones you know.
  • A new value: old total + new value, then ÷ the NEW count. (Not "old mean + new value ÷ 2".) A new value above the mean pulls it up; below pulls it down; equal leaves it the same.

Averages from a frequency table

Matches (f)2521
  • Mean = Σfx ÷ Σf = (0×2 + 1×5 + 2×2 + 3×1) ÷ 10 = 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2 goals.
  • Mode = the value with the biggest frequency = 1 (not 5: that's how many times).
  • Median: 10 values, so halfway between values 5 and 6. Running totals 2, 7… both are 1, so the median is 1.
  • Grouped data (0–10, 10–20…): you don't know the exact values, so use the mid-interval value of each class (5, 15…) and estimate the mean the same way. "10–20" means at least 10 but less than 20.

The charts

Bar chart: compares categories. Gaps between bars, equal widths, a scale that starts at 0.

Pie chart: shares of ONE whole. Same 20 students: 360° ÷ 20 = 18° each, so Bus = 9 × 18° = 162°.

Line plot: one dot per value on a number line. Mode = tallest stack (2); median = middle dots (2).

Histogram: numerical data in equal classes, bars touching, the scale under the bar edges. Modal class here: 160–170.

Stem-and-leaf: tens on the stem, units as leaves, in order, with a key. It keeps every value. 15 values → median is value 8 = 24; range 47 − 12 = 35; mode 24.

Back-to-back: compares two groups on one stem. The left leaves get bigger going outwards (read 5 | 2 as 25). Boys' median 23.5, Girls' 32, so the girls are higher.

Choosing the display and the average

  • Categorical data (words): bar chart or pie chart (pie = parts of a whole). Average: mode only.
  • A few numbers, small values (0 to 6): line plot. Two-digit numbers, keep every value: stem-and-leaf. Comparing two groups: back-to-back stem-and-leaf. Lots of data in classes: histogram.
  • Mean uses every value, but one huge or tiny value (an outlier) drags it. With an outlier, the median is the better typical value (house prices, salaries).
  • When you compare two groups, give one average AND the range for each, then say what they mean in words: "The girls scored higher on average and were less spread out."

Misleading charts and limited samples

Spot the trick. Eoin's bar looks three times as tall, but he got 56 votes and Ciara 52: only 4 more. The vertical axis starts at 50, not 0. Other tricks: uneven gaps on a scale (0, 10, 20, 50, 100), a pie chart whose percentages don't add to 100%, 3-D slices that make the front look bigger, and pictures that grow in two directions at once.
What can a sample tell you? "8 of my 10 friends like the new canteen" is a fact about your 10 friends. It is not evidence that most students in Ireland like it: the sample is small and not random. Say what your data show, and say what limits your conclusion (sample size, who was asked, how the question was worded).

Classic mistakes

  • Finding the median without putting the values in order first.
  • Giving the position of the median ("the 8th value") instead of the value itself.
  • Giving the frequency as the mode (5) instead of the value (1 goal).
  • Dividing Σfx by the number of rows instead of the total frequency.
  • Reading a back-to-back plot's left side the wrong way round (the left leaf 5 next to stem 2 is 25, not 52).
  • Pie chart angles that don't add up to 360° (check!).
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Expect at least one full statistics question, often worth a lot of marks: read or draw a chart (a back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot is a favourite), work out the mean, median, mode and range, compare two groups in words, and judge a chart or a claim. It is also the "analyse" and "interpret" half of CBA 2 (the Statistical Investigation).

How marks are won and lost

  • Show the working: write the total and what you divide by (Σfx = 630, ÷ 30). A right answer with no working can lose marks; working with a slip still earns some.
  • Compare with numbers AND words: "The median for 1st years (27) is higher than for 3rd years (22), so 1st years did more exercise." Saying only "1st years are higher" isn't enough.
  • Charts need a title, labelled axes, equal scales and (for stem-and-leaf) a key, with the leaves in order.
  • Check: pie chart angles add to 360°, frequencies add to the total, the mean lies between the smallest and largest values.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. Saturday exercise. The back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot shows the minutes of exercise some 1st years and 3rd years did on a Saturday.
    (a) Find the median for each group. (b) Find the range for each group. (c) Use your answers to compare the two groups.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 1st years: 13 values, so the 7th = 27 min. 3rd years: 11 values, so the 6th = 22 min.
    (b) 1st years: 45 − 12 = 33 min. 3rd years: 33 − 10 = 23 min.
    (c) The 1st years did more exercise on average (median 27 v 22 minutes), and their times were more spread out (range 33 v 23 minutes).

  2. A pie chart. 72 students named their favourite sport: Hurling 20, Soccer 26, Rugby 8, Camogie 18. (a) Find the angle for each sector of a pie chart. (b) What percentage chose soccer, to 1 decimal place? (c) Ciara says "Soccer is the most popular sport in Ireland." Give one reason why her data can't show this.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 360° ÷ 72 = 5° per student. Hurling 20 × 5 = 100°, Soccer 130°, Rugby 40°, Camogie 90° (check: 360° ✓).
    (b) 26 ÷ 72 × 100 = 36.1%.
    (c) The sample is only 72 students, probably from one school, not a random sample of people in Ireland, so it can only describe those students.

  3. Means. (a) The mean of six numbers is 14. One number is removed and the mean of the other five is 13. Find the number that was removed. (b) The table shows the time 30 students spent on homework: 0–10 min: 4, 10–20 min: 9, 20–30 min: 12, 30–40 min: 5. Estimate the mean time. (c) Why is your answer to (b) only an estimate?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Total of six = 6 × 14 = 84. Total of five = 5 × 13 = 65. Removed: 84 − 65 = 19.
    (b) Mid-interval values 5, 15, 25, 35: (5×4 + 15×9 + 25×12 + 35×5) ÷ 30 = 630 ÷ 30 = 21 minutes.
    (c) We only know the class each time is in, not the exact times, so we use the middle of each class.

  4. A misleading chart. The bar chart in the rules above (votes for class rep) has a vertical axis from 50 to 57. Ciara got 52 votes and Eoin got 56. (a) How many more votes did Eoin get? (b) Eoin says "The chart shows I got three times as many votes as Ciara." Explain why he is wrong. (c) How should the chart be redrawn?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) 56 − 52 = 4 votes.
    (b) His bar is three times as tall only because the axis starts at 50 (his bar shows 6 above 50, hers 2). 56 is not three times 52; he got about 8% more.
    (c) Start the vertical axis at 0 (with equal steps), so the bar heights are in proportion to the votes.
