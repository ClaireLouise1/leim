Poetry 1: imagery & sound

Poets paint pictures (imagery) and play with sound. Spot the technique, then say what it does. The second part wins the marks.

Spot the pattern

Three lines

1The moon hung like a silver coin.

2The moon is a lamp above our town.

3The moon frowned down from behind a cloud.

All three lines are about the moon. Which two compare it to something? What's the one small word that's different between those two? What is the moon doing in line 3 that a moon can't really do?

Show me

Lines 1 and 2 compare the moon to something. Line 1 uses like: that's a simile. Line 2 says the moon is a lamp: that's a metaphor.
Line 3: only a person can frown. Giving a thing a human action is personification. It makes the moon seem cross or gloomy.

The six techniques

🤝 Similecompares with like or as
Rain fell like tiny needles.
🪞 Metaphorsays one thing is another
The classroom was a zoo.
🗣️ Personificationa thing acts like a person
The wind whispered.
🔤 Alliterationsame first sound, close together
Fierce flames flickered.
💥 Onomatopoeiathe word sounds like the noise
sizzle, crash, creak
🎵 Rhymesame end sound
night / bright (not night / eight)

Imagery: the five senses

👀 Sightcolours, light, shapes
👂 Soundroared, echoed, rang
👃 Smellscent, stank, perfume
👅 Tastesour, salty, bitter
✋ Touchrough, icy, soft
  • Name it, quote it, explain it. “The wind whispered” is personification: it makes the wind sound gentle and secret.
  • Ask “So what?” What picture, mood or feeling does the technique create?
  • Rhyme is about sound, not spelling: food and good look alike but don't rhyme.
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Poetry is a regular part of the Junior Cycle paper: a poem you studied, or an unseen poem printed on the paper. Questions ask what you notice about the language, the sounds and the mood, and how the poet creates them.

How marks are won and lost

  • Naming a technique is only the start. Most marks go for explaining its effect.
  • Quote the exact words, short: three to six words.
  • Link to the mood or meaning of the whole poem: calm, lonely, exciting, sad.
  • Don't list every technique you can find. Two well-explained points beat five names.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

The Harbour at Night
The boats are sleeping side by side,
their ropes creak softly on the tide.
The lighthouse blinks its yellow eye,
a lonely star in a black sky.
Gulls grumble on the gleaming wall,
and slap, slap, slap, the waters fall.

  1. Find one example of personification in the poem and explain what it adds.
    Show a model answer

    “The boats are sleeping side by side”. Boats can't sleep, so the poet gives them a human action. It makes the harbour feel peaceful and safe, like children tucked up in bed.
    Where the marks would go: a correct quote, the technique named, and what it makes us feel or picture.

  2. Pick out one sound word (onomatopoeia). What does it help you hear?
    Show a model answer

    “slap, slap, slap”: you can hear the small waves hitting the harbour wall. Repeating it three times gives a slow, steady rhythm, like the sea breathing.
    Where the marks would go: the sound word, what you hear, and one comment on the effect.

  3. The lighthouse is called “a lonely star in a black sky”. What does this metaphor suggest?
    Show a model answer

    It suggests the lighthouse is the only bright thing in the dark, like one star in an empty sky. “Lonely” adds a sad, quiet feeling: it is alone, watching over the boats all night.
    Where the marks would go: what is compared, the picture it creates, and a comment on one key word (lonely).

  4. How does the poet make the poem sound calm? Refer to two techniques.
    Show a model answer

    1. Rhyming couplets (side / tide, eye / sky, wall / fall) give it a gentle, regular beat, like a lullaby.
    2. Soft sounds: the ropes “creak softly” and the alliteration in “gulls grumble on the gleaming wall” is low and murmuring, not sharp or loud.
    Where the marks would go: two separate techniques, each with a quote and a link to the calm mood.
