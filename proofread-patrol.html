Editing & redrafting: proofreading your own work

Proofreading means reading your own writing again to catch the slips: spelling, mix-up words, grammar and punctuation. Redrafting goes further: you make the writing better, not just correct. Both are part of the Junior Cycle, and both win marks.

Spot the pattern

✏️ First draftMy brother and me went to the beach. Their was nobody else there. We should of brought a coat because it was freezeing.
✅ ProofreadMy brother and I went to the beach. There was nobody else there. We should have brought a coat because it was freezing.

Four slips in three sentences. What kind of slip is each one? Which would a spell checker miss?

Show me

me → I is grammar (take away "my brother": I went). Their → There and of → have are mix-up words: real words in the wrong place. freezeing → freezing is spelling (drop the e before -ing). A spell checker would only catch the last one, because the other three are real words. That's why you have to read it yourself.

Four passes: what to look for

1. 🔤 SpellingYour own tricky words (definitely, separate, beginning). Read slowly, word by word.
2. 🔀 Mix-upsthere / their / they're, to / too, then / than, should have (not of).
3. ⚙️ GrammarSame tense all the way, subject and verb agree (each of them has), I or me, saw or seen.
4. ✒️ PunctuationCapitals, full stops (no run-ons), apostrophes, speech marks.

Tips: read it in your head as if you were reading it aloud; you hear slips you don't see. Keep a list of your own usual slips and check for those first. Leave a couple of minutes at the end of every answer to read it back.

Fewer or less? Who or whom?

⚖️ fewer / lessfewer for things you can count: fewer cars, fewer goals.
less for things you can't: less milk, less time, less noise.
👤 who / whomwho does the action: the girl who won (she won).
whom has it done to them: the man whom we met (we met him), and after to / with / for: to whom.

Memory trick: him and whom both end in m. If you'd say him, write whom.

Brackets and dashes

Brackets ( ) or a pair of dashes – – hold extra information in the middle of a sentence. Take it out and the sentence still works:

Our dog (a very lazy spaniel) slept through the storm.
Our dog – a very lazy spaniel – slept through the storm.

Always use them in pairs, and don't add commas as well.

Redrafting: make it better

DraftThe match was good. It was good because the crowd was good and the goals were good.
RedraftThe match was thrilling. A roaring crowd watched four goals, each one better than the last.

When you redraft: cut words you've repeated, swap weak words for stronger ones (Word Ladder), start sentences in different ways, and check each paragraph has one main idea (Paragraph Builder). In your Collection of Texts (CBA 2) you show your redrafting, so keep your drafts.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

There's rarely a question that says "proofread this", but every written answer is marked partly for accuracy: spelling, grammar and punctuation. The students who keep those marks are the ones who leave time to read their answers back. A careful read can catch four or five slips in a page.

How marks are won and lost

  • Plan your time so you have a couple of minutes at the end of each longer answer to read it back.
  • Check for your own usual slips first (there / their, should of, a missing full stop).
  • Cross out neatly with one line and write the fix above: examiners don't mind neat corrections.
  • Watch the tense: a story that slips from past to present loses marks.
  • Don't rewrite a whole answer at the end: fix the slips and move on.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

Last Saturday my cousin and me went to the races in Galway. We should of left earlier because their was a huge queue at the gate. Each of the horses were beautiful. We stayed untill the last race. I definately want to go back next year.
  1. The paragraph above has six slips. Find each one and write the correction.
    Show a model answer

    me → I (my cousin and I went); of → have (should have); their → there (there was); were → was (each of the horses was); untill → until; definately → definitely.
    Where the marks would go: one mark for each slip found and fixed; a short reason helps (e.g. "each = one, so was").

  2. Fill the gaps: (a) There were ___ people on the bus today. (fewer / less) (b) We had ___ rain this summer. (fewer / less) (c) The woman ___ we helped sent us a card. (who / whom)
    Show a model answer

    (a) fewer: you can count people. (b) less: you can't count rain. (c) whom: we helped her, so whom.
    Where the marks would go: the right word in each gap, with the reason.

  3. Add brackets or a pair of dashes to this sentence: My uncle a fisherman from Kinsale caught a huge crab.
    Show a model answer

    My uncle (a fisherman from Kinsale) caught a huge crab. or My uncle – a fisherman from Kinsale – caught a huge crab. Take the extra information out and the sentence still works: My uncle caught a huge crab.
    Where the marks would go: a pair of marks round exactly the extra information.

  4. Redraft this paragraph to make it better: The walk was nice. We walked up the hill and it was nice. At the top the view was nice and we had a nice picnic.
    Show a model answer

    The walk up Knocknarea was steep but worth every step. At the top, the whole of Sligo Bay spread out below us, silver in the sun. We sat on the cairn and ate squashed sandwiches that tasted like a feast.
    Where the marks would go: no repeated "nice"; stronger, more exact words; detail you can picture; sentences that start in different ways; still the same walk.
