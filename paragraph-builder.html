Paragraphs and topic sentences

A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main idea. Good paragraphs make your writing easy to follow, and that earns marks in every piece you write.

Spot the pattern

These four sentences are one paragraph, but they're jumbled. Which one should go first?

1. Every spring, thousands of puffins arrive on the Saltee Islands.
2. Their bright orange beaks make them look really unusual.
3. Puffins are some of the most unusual birds in Ireland.
4. These birds dig burrows in the soft soil at the top of the cliffs.

Which sentence could be a title for all the others? Which words does it share with them?

Show me

3 is the topic sentence. It gives the main idea, and every other sentence links back to it: puffins (1), unusual (2), birds (4). The other three are supporting sentences: they give details, facts or examples.

How a paragraph is built

⭐ Topic sentencethe main idea, usually first
Dogs are loyal and loving pets.
🧱 Supporting sentencesdetails, facts, examples, reasons
A dog will often wait by the door for its owner.
🔗 Linka last line that sums up or leads into the next paragraph

One paragraph = one main idea. A sentence about something else doesn't belong: move it or cut it.

When to start a new paragraph: TiPToP

⏰ Timetime moves on
The next morning, …
📍 Placea new place
Back at home, …
💡 Topica new point or idea
Another reason …
👤 Persona new person comes in or speaks
Mr Byrne stood up.

In handwriting, show a new paragraph by leaving a line or starting the first line further in (indenting).

Linking words

🔢 OrderFirst · Next · Then · After that · Finally · In the end
➕ AddingIn addition · Also · What's more
↔️ ContrastHowever · On the other hand
👉 ExampleFor example · For instance
➡️ ResultAs a result · Because of this
  • Put a comma after these words at the start of a sentence: However, the rooms were not clean.
  • Don't start every sentence with one. One or two per paragraph is plenty.
  • In an argument, start each paragraph with a new point: Firstly … Secondly … Finally …
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Every writing task (a personal essay, a story, an article, a speech, a letter) is marked partly on how well it is organised. Clear paragraphs, each with one main idea and a strong topic sentence, are the quickest way to show that. Reading questions can also ask how a writer has structured a piece.

How marks are won and lost

  • One long block of writing is hard to follow and loses marks for structure.
  • A clear topic sentence tells the examiner your point straight away.
  • Support every point with a detail, example or reason. A topic sentence on its own is not a paragraph.
  • Link your paragraphs (However, As a result, In addition) so your answer flows.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. Write a topic sentence for this paragraph: “… The 100 metres race was won by a first year. Ms Walsh's relay team came last but had the loudest cheers. The rain held off until the very last event.”
    Show a model answer

    Our school sports day was full of surprises this year.
    Where the marks would go: one sentence that sums up all three details (the race, the relay, the weather) without repeating one of them.

  2. Read this paragraph. Which sentence doesn't belong, and where should a new paragraph start? Use TiPToP to explain.
    We reached the campsite just as it got dark. Dad put up the tent while we collected sticks. My cousin has a new bike. The next morning, we woke to the sound of rain on the roof.
    Show the answer

    “My cousin has a new bike.” doesn't belong: it has nothing to do with the camping trip. A new paragraph should start at “The next morning,” because time moves on (the T in TiPToP).
    Where the marks would go: the right sentence + why it's off-topic; the right place + the TiPToP reason.

  3. Fill each gap with a linking word: “Our town needs a skate park. (a) ___, teenagers have nowhere safe to go in the evenings. (b) ___, the skate park in Clonakilty is busy every night. (c) ___, the council should build one this year.”
    Show the answer

    (a) Firstly / First of all (b) For example (c) As a result / For these reasons
    Where the marks would go: a linking word that fits the job of each sentence (a point, an example, a conclusion), with a comma after it.

  4. “Students should not have to wear a school uniform.” Write one well-built paragraph for or against this view.
    Show a model answer

    School uniforms save families money and stress. A uniform costs less than the new clothes many students would want to wear every week. For example, a jumper and two shirts can last a whole year. In addition, nobody is judged on what brand they are wearing, so mornings are calmer. For these reasons, uniforms should stay.
    Where the marks would go: a clear topic sentence (the point), supporting sentences with a reason and an example, linking words, and a last line that sums up.

Came up in: not a question of its own, but every longer written answer on the 2022–2026 papers needed well-organised paragraphs. See the past papers ↗.
