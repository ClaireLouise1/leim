The paper explained

Junior Cycle English is one written paper in June of 3rd year, at Higher or Ordinary level, set and marked by the State Examinations Commission.

  • 2 hours and 180 marks
  • The whole paper is built around one theme (for example "Perception" or "Journeys and Adventure"), and every text and question links to it
  • It mixes unseen texts (a poem, a story extract, an article, a poster…), questions on texts you studied (your novel, poems, play or film) and writing tasks (a story, a speech, a letter, a review…)
  • The sections and marks change every year, so you can't learn the layout off. What doesn't change is the skills it tests

You write in an answer booklet, in the spaces under each question. The marks for each question are printed beside it: use them (see Timing).

Timing: let the marks decide

2 hours for 180 marks works out at ⅔ of a minute (40 seconds) a mark. So the marks beside a question tell you how long it deserves:

MarksAbout this long
10 marks7 minutes
15 marks10 minutes
20 marks13 minutes
25 marks17 minutes
30 marks20 minutes
40 marks27 minutes
60 marks40 minutes
The trap: spending 25 minutes on a 10-mark question you love, then rushing a 40-mark writing task. When your time for a question is up, finish the sentence and move on. You can come back.

Practise this with the Exam technique round in English Exam Mix: it asks you "minutes for this question?" against the clock.

What is the question really asking?

The first word or two of a question tell you what kind of answer gets the marks.

If it says…Do this
Identify, name, list, pick out, findA short answer with the exact detail or words from the text. No long explaining.
DescribeSay what it's like, with details from the text.
Explain, how, whySay why or how. Use "because", and back it up with the text.
Compare, contrastSay how the two are alike and how they're different. Mention both, every time.
Do you agree, in your opinion, which do you preferSay clearly what you think, then give reasons from the text.
Summarise, sum upThe main points only, short, in your own words.

For a writing task, find three things before you write a word: the form (article, speech, letter, diary, review…), the reader (your principal, your class, a magazine's readers) and the purpose (to persuade, describe, inform, entertain). A brilliant story written when the task asked for a speech will lose marks.

What makes a good answer

This is our guide, based on how English answers are usually judged; it's not the official marking scheme. Good answers:

  1. Answer the exact question, in the first sentence. Use its words.
  2. Make a point, back it with a short quote, then explain it (point, quote, explain). A quote of 2–6 words, in quotation marks, is better than a copied-out paragraph.
  3. Explain more than you retell. "This shows…", "This makes the reader feel…", "The writer does this because…" are where the marks are.
  4. Match the marks. A 10-mark question usually wants two or three well-made points; a 30-mark one wants more, and more development.
  5. Get the form right in writing tasks, and write in clear paragraphs with careful spelling and punctuation. Leave two minutes to read it back.

The SEC publishes every marking scheme free, so you can see exactly how a past question was marked (see For parents).

Model answers

Three exam-style questions on texts written by Léim. Try each one first (write it or say it out loud), then open the model answer.

Unseen poem · Higher level style

Sound in a poem

"Wet Break" (written by Léim)

Rain drums the roof like a hundred fingers, the windows sweat, the radiators tick. Someone's crisp packet crackles like a fire and thirty voices climb, louder, quick, till the door swings open. Silence. Click.

How does the poet use sound to bring the wet break to life? Support your answer with reference to the poem.

Show the model answer
The poet fills the poem with sound to show how loud and restless a wet break is. In the first line the rain "drums the roof like a hundred fingers". This simile makes the rain sound constant and impatient, as if it is tapping to be let in, so the poem is noisy from the very start. The poet also uses onomatopoeia: the radiators "tick" and a crisp packet "crackles like a fire". These small, sharp sounds let me hear the room, and comparing a crisp packet to a fire is funny, because it shows how bored and jumpy everyone is. The noise builds when "thirty voices climb, louder, quick", and the short words and commas make the line itself speed up. Then the poet stops everything with two one-word sentences: "Silence. Click." After so much noise, the sudden quiet tells us someone, probably a teacher, has come in, and the full stops make the reader stop too.

Why it works

  • It answers the question in the first sentence: sound, and what it shows.
  • Every point has a short quote (a few words, not whole lines) and then explains what the quote does.
  • It names techniques (simile, onomatopoeia) but always says why they matter. Naming a technique on its own earns little.
  • It follows the poem from start to finish, so the answer has a shape: noise builds, then stops.

Unseen prose · Ordinary level style

What is a character like?

Gran never sat down. Even while the kettle was boiling she was wiping a counter that was already clean, or straightening the photographs on the dresser. When I came in soaked from the match, she tutted, threw a towel at my head and had a plate of toast in front of me before I'd taken off my boots.

"You'll catch your death," she said. But she was smiling.

What kind of person is Gran? Give two qualities and support each one with evidence from the extract.

Show the model answer

1. Gran is hard-working, maybe restless. The writer says she "never sat down" and was wiping a counter "that was already clean". This shows she always has to be busy, even when there is nothing left to do.

2. Gran is caring, even though she hides it. She "tutted" and "threw a towel" at the narrator, which sounds cross, but she had toast ready "before I'd taken off my boots". Her words are a telling-off, but "she was smiling", so we know she is really glad to look after her grandchild.

Why it works

  • Two clear qualities, one per paragraph, each in bold, so the examiner can see both straight away.
  • Point, quote, explain: the quote is short and the explanation says what it shows about her.
  • The second point notices a contrast (cross words, kind actions). Spotting that is what lifts an answer.

Writing · Higher or Ordinary

The opening of a talk

Write the text of a talk for your class on why every student should learn to cook before they leave school. (The opening is shown.)

Show the model answer

Good morning, everyone. Quick question: hands up if you could cook a dinner for four people tonight, without a packet, a microwave or a video telling you what to do.

Be honest.

When I asked our class last week, six hands went up. Six, out of twenty-eight. In a few years most of us will be living away from home, in college, in a job or in a flat with people we barely know. And right now, most of us couldn't boil an egg without looking it up.

So today I want to convince you of one thing: learning to cook isn't a hobby. It's a life skill, like swimming or reading a bus timetable, and every student in this school should learn it before they leave. I've got three reasons, and the first one is money.

Why it works

  • It's written as a talk: it greets the audience, asks them to do something and speaks to "you".
  • The hook is a question the audience can answer, then a fact from the speaker's own class survey.
  • The opinion is clear by the end of the opening, and "three reasons, the first one is money" tells the listener what's coming.
  • Short sentences ("Be honest.") are used on purpose, for effect, the way a speaker would pause.

Studied texts: get ready

Questions on your studied novel, poems, play and film almost always ask you to talk about a key moment or a character, and to back it up. For each text, have ready:

  • 2–3 key moments you can describe in a few lines: what happens, and why it matters
  • The main characters: what they're like, how they change, and a short quote or detail for each
  • The theme(s), in a sentence each, so you can link any text to the paper's theme
  • For a film: two or three techniques (a shot, the music, lighting) in one key scene
  • Higher level: Shakespeare came up every year from 2023 to 2026 (see What came up)

CBAs (Classroom-Based Assessments)

English CBAs are done in class, judged by your teacher, and reported on your Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement, separately from the June grade.

CBA 1: Oral Communication

A talk of about 3 minutes, alone or in a group. Choosing a topic, a 3-week plan, cue cards, how it's judged and the reflection note.

Read the guide →

CBA 2: Collection of Texts

At least four pieces of your writing in four genres; you pick two. Genres, redrafting, how it's judged and the reflection note.

Read the guide →
This year's rule (exams in 2027 and 2028): every student does at least one English CBA, and the school decides whether it's one or both. Ask your teacher which one your class is doing, and when. The written Assessment Task is not examined, so the June exam is 100% of the English grade. Dates and rules for this school year →

Grades

Your marks out of 180 become a percentage, and the percentage gives the grade. These are the grade bands used since the 2025 exams, the same at both levels. The marks are rounded.

GradePercentageAbout this many marks out of 180
Distinction85–100%153–180
Higher Merit70–84%126–152
Merit55–69%99–125
Achieved40–54%72–98
Partially Achieved20–39%36–71
Not Graded0–19%0–35

That means 18 marks is 10%: one 20-mark question left half-done can be the difference between two grades.

For parents

  • The June exam gives the grade. It's one 2-hour paper, at Higher or Ordinary level, set and marked by the State Examinations Commission.
  • The CBAs are separate. They're judged by the class teacher and appear on the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement. CBAs explained, with example work.
  • Talk about the texts. Asking "what was the best moment in your novel, and why?" over dinner is real revision: it's exactly what the paper asks.
  • Try one question against the clock. Past papers and marking schemes are free on the SEC's exam archive ↗ (tick the box to accept their terms, then choose Exam Papers, the year, Junior Cycle and English). Use the marks to set the time, then check the answer against the marking scheme together.
