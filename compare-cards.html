Comparing texts and characters

A comparing question gives you two texts (two poems, two extracts, or two texts you studied) and asks how they are alike and how they are different. The trick is to write about both texts together, point by point, instead of one essay on each.

Spot the pattern

Answer 1: “The Calf” is about Ruth in a barn at night. She gives her coat to a calf and she grins at the end. “Inis Oírr” is about Nuala on a ferry. She gives her seat to an old man and she laughs.Answer 2: Both main characters are kind: Ruth “shared her coat” and Nuala “offered her seat”. However, “The Calf” is set on a farm at night, whereas “Inis Oírr” is set by the sea in bright sunshine.

Both answers use the same two texts. Which one is really comparing? What does it do that the other doesn't?

Show me

Answer 2. Answer 1 retells one text, then the other, and leaves the reader to find the links. Answer 2 names a similarity (both kind, with a short quote from each), then a difference (setting), and the linking words (Both, However, whereas) do the comparing out loud.

What to compare

  • Narrator: first person (“I”) or third person (he / she)?
  • Setting: where and when? (by the sea, on a farm, at night, in sunshine)
  • Main character (the game calls them the hero): what are they like? (kind, brave, determined, honest) Back it up with a quote from each text.
  • Mood and ending: does it end happily or sadly? Does the mood change?
  • Theme: what is each text about underneath (friendship, loss, courage)? Two texts with the same theme can still feel very different.

Linking words

🤝 AlikeBoth … · Similarly, … · Likewise, … · In the same way, … · also · too
↔️ DifferentHowever, … · In contrast, … · On the other hand, … · whereas · while · unlike

A useful frame: Both texts … (similarity + a quote from each) → However, … or …, whereas … (difference + a quote from each) → Overall, I prefer … because … (only if the question asks).

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

The paper often puts two texts on the same theme side by side (two poems, a poem and an extract, two short pieces) and asks you to compare them. On Higher Level especially, questions on your studied texts can also ask you to compare two of them (for example a novel and a film, or two poems you studied). Marks go to points that link both texts, evidence from each and clear linking words, not to two separate summaries.

How marks are won and lost

  • Write about both texts in every paragraph: point → text A → text B.
  • Quote from both: one short quote each is better than three from one text.
  • Use linking words (both, however, whereas) so the comparison is clear.
  • Similarities and differences, unless the question asks for only one.
  • Don't retell either text, and don't spend all your time on the one you like better.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Both extracts are original. "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

Text A: The Last Match I laced my boots in the empty dressing room in Ballina. It was my last game for the under-16s, and the evening light was already fading. When the whistle went, I ran until my lungs burned. We lost by two points. Walking off, I looked back at the pitch for a long time.
Text B: First Swim Every summer morning, Gran swam at the Forty Foot. This year, for the first time, Kate went with her. The water was so cold that Kate gasped and grabbed the steps. Gran just laughed and held out her hand. By the end of the week, Kate was diving in first.
  1. Name one way the two texts are similar. Support your answer with a short quote from each text.
    Show a model answer

    Both main characters push themselves physically. In Text A the narrator “ran until my lungs burned”, and in Text B Kate keeps going into the freezing water until she is “diving in first”. In both texts, effort shows what the character is like.
    Where the marks would go: a clear similarity; a short quote from each text; a sentence linking them.

  2. Name two ways the texts are different.
    Show a model answer

    (1) Narrator: Text A is told in the first person (“I laced my boots”), whereas Text B is told in the third person (“Kate went with her”). (2) Ending: Text A ends sadly, with a loss and a long look back at the pitch, while Text B ends happily, with Kate “diving in first”. (Also fair: an ending vs a beginning; alone vs with Gran; evening vs morning.)
    Where the marks would go: two different points; evidence for each text; a contrast word (whereas, while, however).

  3. Both texts are about a moment that matters. Which text shows the character's feelings more clearly, in your opinion? Refer to both texts.
    Show a model answer

    Sample: Text A, for me. The narrator never says “I was sad”, but “I looked back at the pitch for a long time” shows how hard it is to let go. Text B tells us Kate “gasped and grabbed the steps”, which shows her shock, but we see her more from the outside, through her actions with Gran. However, Text B shows a bigger change in its character.
    Where the marks would go: a clear opinion; a quote from each text; a reason that compares them (not just praise for one).

  4. Compare two texts you studied (for example two poems, or your novel and your film) on one theme. Plan your answer.
    Show a model answer

    Plan for any two texts: (1) Opening line naming both texts and the theme. (2) Similarity: how both texts show the theme, with a short quote or key moment from each. (3) Difference: narrator, setting, mood, ending or the way the main character changes, with evidence from each, using “whereas” or “However”. (4) A second difference or similarity. (5) A closing sentence: which text shows the theme more powerfully, and why.
    Where the marks would go: both texts in every paragraph; evidence from each; clear linking words; a personal response if asked.
