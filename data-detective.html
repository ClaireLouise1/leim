Statistics: questions, data & tables

Spot the pattern

Look at the answers in each list. Could a person have an answer in between two of them?

  • Number of brothers and sisters: 0, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1
  • Height: 1.52 m, 1.61 m, 1.48 m, 1.57 m
  • Goals scored in a match: 0, 3, 1, 2
  • Time to run 100 m: 13.2 s, 14.05 s, 12.87 s

Which lists can only be whole numbers? Which could be 1.555 m or 13.4172 s? What is the difference in how the data were got?

Show me

Nobody has 2.5 brothers and you can't score 1.5 goals: those answers are counted, so they are discrete. Height and time are measured, so any value in a range is possible (a better stopwatch just gives more decimal places): they are continuous. Ask yourself: did I count it or measure it?

The four types of data

Categorical (words or categories)
Nominal: no natural order. Favourite sport, eye colour, county, how you travel to school.
Ordinal: a natural order. T-shirt size (S, M, L), mild / medium / hot, strongly agree → strongly disagree, star ratings.
Numerical (numbers)
Discrete: counted, only certain values. Number of siblings, goals, text messages.
Continuous: measured, any value in a range. Height, mass, time, temperature, distance.

Asking a statistical question

A statistical question expects the answers to vary, so you need to collect data to answer it. "How many hours of sleep do students in my class get?" is statistical. "How tall am I?" or "What time does school start?" have one fixed answer, so they are not.

Population, sample and census

  • The population is everyone (or everything) you want to know about: all 650 students in the school.
  • A sample is the smaller group you actually ask: 40 of those students.
  • A census collects data from every member of the population. (Ireland's Census asks every household.)
  • A random sample gives everyone the same chance of being picked: names out of a hat, a random number generator, every 10th name on the full list.

Bias: keep it fair

Biased sample. Asking only one kind of person. To find out how much exercise 2nd years do, asking the basketball team, or people coming out of the gym, gives results that are too high.
Leading question. A question that pushes people towards an answer: "Don't you agree that homework is a waste of time?", "Surely…", "Everyone knows…", "How much do you love…?". Fair version: "How do you feel about the amount of homework you get?"
Answer boxes. For "How many books did you read last year?": 0–2 · 3–5 · 6–8 · 9 or more. No overlaps (0–2, 2–4 puts 2 in two boxes), no gaps, and every answer has a box, including 0 and big numbers.

Collecting the data

  • Primary data: you collect it yourself. Secondary data: someone else already collected it (Met Éireann, the CSO, a website, a newspaper).
  • Survey / questionnaire: ask people. Observation: watch and count (cars passing the gate). Experiment: change one thing and measure what happens (paper planes with bigger wings). Existing data: look it up.
The four stages of a statistical investigation: 1. Pose a question → 2. Collect the data → 3. Analyse the data (tables, charts) → 4. Interpret the results (answer your question, and say what limits your answer). This is exactly what you do in CBA 2.

Tally marks and frequency tables

Go through the raw data once, making a tally mark for each answer (cross it off so it isn't counted twice). Every fifth mark goes across the other four, so you can count in fives. The frequency is how many times each answer comes up. The frequencies add up to the total number asked.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

This is the start of every statistics question, and it is what CBA 2 (the Statistical Investigation) is built on. Expect to name the type of data, say whether a sample or question is biased and how to fix it, fill in a frequency table from raw data, and suggest a question or a way to collect data for an investigation. (Charts and averages come later, in Statistics 2.)

How marks are won and lost

  • Use both words. "Categorical" alone is half an answer: say categorical nominal or categorical ordinal, numerical discrete or numerical continuous.
  • Give a reason. "Biased" earns little on its own; say who is left out ("only asks people at the gym, who probably exercise more").
  • Check the total. The frequencies must add up to the number of people asked.
  • Make questions answerable. A fair, clear question with answer boxes that don't overlap and include every answer.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. Name the data. Say what type of data each gives: (a) the number of goals in each match, (b) each student's favourite type of music, (c) the time each student takes to solve a puzzle, (d) the size of pizza each customer orders (small, medium, large).
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Numerical discrete (counted). (b) Categorical nominal (no order). (c) Numerical continuous (measured). (d) Categorical ordinal (small → medium → large).

  2. A cycle lane survey. Cork City Council wants to know what adults in the city think of a new cycle lane. A worker asks 50 people cycling in the lane one morning. (a) What is the population? (b) Explain why the sample is biased. (c) Describe a better way to choose the sample. (d) Rewrite this question so it is fair: "Isn't the new cycle lane a great idea?"
    Show the worked answer

    (a) All adults living in Cork city.
    (b) People cycling in the lane are likely to like it; drivers, walkers and people who don't use it are left out.
    (c) Pick adults at random from a list of everyone in the city, at different times and places, and ask more of them.
    (d) For example: "What do you think of the new cycle lane?" with boxes from "very good" to "very poor".

  3. Tally it up. 20 students said how they travel to school: W, B, B, C, W, B, Bi, W, B, C, B, W, B, Bi, B, W, C, B, W, B (W = walk, B = bus, C = car, Bi = bike). (a) Make a frequency table with a tally column. (b) What fraction of the students take the bus? (c) What type of data is this?
    Show the worked answer

    (a) Walk 6, Bus 9, Car 3, Bike 2 (total 20 ✓).
    (b) 9/20.
    (c) Categorical nominal.

  4. Plan an investigation. Saoirse thinks students who play sport sleep longer than students who don't. (a) Write a statistical question she could investigate. (b) What data should she collect, and what type is each? (c) How could she choose a fair sample from her school of 600? (d) Name one stage that comes after collecting the data.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) For example: "Do students in my school who play sport sleep longer on school nights than students who don't?"
    (b) Whether each student plays sport (categorical nominal: yes / no) and how long they slept (numerical continuous).
    (c) A random sample, e.g. 60 names picked with a random number generator from the full school list, with every year group included.
    (d) Analyse the data (tables and charts), then interpret the results.
