Lines, angles & triangles

Spot the pattern

Draw any triangle on paper: long and thin, fat, or with one big angle. Colour the three corners, tear them off and put the three points together, side by side.

  • What shape do the three corners make when you line them up?
  • Does it matter what triangle you started with?
  • So what must the three angles of every triangle add up to?
Show me

The three corners always fit together to make a straight line, and a straight angle is 180°. So the three angles of any triangle add up to 180°. If you know two of them, you can always find the third: 180° minus the other two.

Angle words

Acute: less than 90°. Right angle: exactly 90° (marked with a little square). Obtuse: between 90° and 180°. Straight angle: exactly 180°. Reflex: more than 180° but less than 360°.
Naming an angle: ∠ABC means the angle at B: the middle letter is always the vertex (the corner where the two arms meet).
Lines: a line segment has two end points; a ray starts at a point and goes on forever one way. Parallel lines never meet (marked with matching arrows). Perpendicular lines meet at 90°. A transversal is a line that cuts across two or more lines.
Triangles by sides: scalene (all sides different), isosceles (two sides equal, marked with dashes), equilateral (all three equal, so every angle is 60°). By angles: look at the biggest angle: acute-angled (all less than 90°), right-angled (one is 90°), obtuse-angled (one is more than 90°).

The angle facts (learn these, with their names)

Angles on a straight line add to 180°. a + b = 180°
Angles at a point add to 360°. a + b + c = 360°
Vertically opposite angles are equal (Theorem 1). a = b
Angles in a triangle add to 180° (Theorem 4). a + b + c = 180°
Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal (Theorem 2). a = b. It works backwards too: two equal angles mean two equal sides.
An exterior angle = the sum of the two interior opposite angles (Theorem 6). e = a + b
Alternate angles are equal (Theorem 3). Between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. a = b
Corresponding angles are equal (Theorem 5). In the same position at each parallel line. a = b
Co-interior angles add to 180°. Between the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal. a + b = 180° (this follows from corresponding angles and a straight line).

You might hear "Z angles" for alternate and "F angles" for corresponding. They help you spot them, but in the exam always write the proper name: alternate, corresponding.

How to chase an angle

  1. Mark every angle you know on the diagram.
  2. Look for a fact that links an angle you know to one you don't: a straight line, a crossing, a triangle, equal sides, parallel arrows.
  3. Write each step on its own line with the reason in brackets: y = 180° − 72° = 108° (straight line).
  4. Keep going until you reach the angle you were asked for. Check: do the angles in each triangle add to 180°? Does the answer look the right size?
Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Angle facts are on the course for both Higher and Ordinary level, and they lead into the later geometry topics (constructions, theorems, congruent and similar triangles, trigonometry). Expect to find missing angles in diagrams with crossing lines, triangles and parallel lines, and to give a reason for each one.

How marks are won and lost

  • The reason is part of the answer. When the question says "give a reason" or "justify", the number on its own doesn't earn full marks. Write the fact: "alternate angles", "angles in a triangle add to 180°".
  • Use the proper names. "Z angles" and "F angles" aren't accepted as reasons.
  • Show each step. If you make a slip early on, clear working means you can still get marks for the later steps.
  • Don't measure. Exam diagrams are usually not drawn to scale. Work it out from the facts.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). Try each one on paper first.

  1. Crossing lines. Two straight lines cross. One of the angles is 58°. Find the angles a, b and c, giving a reason for each.
    Show the worked answer

    a = 180° − 58° = 122° (angles on a straight line).
    b = 58° (vertically opposite angles are equal).
    c = 122° (vertically opposite to a; or 180° − 58° on a straight line).
    Check: 58 + 122 + 58 + 122 = 360 ✓ (angles at a point).

  2. Parallel lines. The two lines with arrows are parallel. Find p, q and r, giving a reason for each.
    Show the worked answer

    p = 65° (corresponding angles are equal).
    q = 180° − 65° = 115° (angles on a straight line).
    r = 115° (alternate angles: r and q are between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal).
    Check: p + r = 65 + 115 = 180 ✓ (co-interior angles).

  3. An isosceles triangle. In the triangle ABC, |AB| = |AC| and ∠BAC = 40°. The side BC is extended to D. (a) Find x, the size of ∠ABC. (b) Find y, the size of ∠ACD. Give a reason for each step.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) |AB| = |AC|, so ∠ABC = ∠ACB (base angles of an isosceles triangle). The angles add to 180°: 180° − 40° = 140°, and 140° ÷ 2 = 70°. So x = 70°.
    (b) y = 180° − 70° = 110° (angles on a straight line at C). Or: y = 40° + 70° = 110° (exterior angle = sum of the two interior opposite angles) ✓.

  4. Two ways. In the triangle PQR, ∠QPR = 48° and ∠PQR = 67°. QR is extended to S. (a) Find w, the size of ∠PRS, using the exterior angle theorem. (b) Find w a different way, to check your answer. (c) Is the triangle acute-angled, right-angled or obtuse-angled? Explain.
    Show the worked answer

    (a) w = 48° + 67° = 115° (an exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles).
    (b) ∠PRQ = 180° − (48° + 67°) = 65° (angles in a triangle), then w = 180° − 65° = 115° (straight line) ✓.
    (c) The angles are 48°, 67° and 65°. All are less than 90°, so it is acute-angled.
