Persuasive writing & speeches

Persuasive writing tries to make the reader agree with you or do something. A speech, a letter to a newspaper, an article or a blog post can all be persuasive.

Spot the pattern

🅰️ Opening AToday I'm going to talk about the skate park. I think we should get one. It would be good.
🅱️ Opening BWhere can a teenager go in this town on a Saturday? Our survey of 200 students found that 150 have nowhere to meet. You can change that tonight.

Both openings want a skate park. Which one would make the council listen? Find three things Opening B does that Opening A doesn't.

Show me

Opening B starts with a rhetorical question (it makes the listener think), gives a statistic (it sounds proven) and uses direct address (“You can change that”). Opening A just announces the topic and gives an opinion with no reason: “It would be good” persuades nobody.

Six techniques

❓ Rhetorical questionWho wants a town with nowhere safe to play?
3️⃣ Rule of threeSport teaches teamwork, patience and respect.
📊 StatisticOur survey found that 8 in 10 students skip breakfast.
💔 Emotive languageInnocent animals are left cold and frightened.
👉 Direct addressYour vote can change this town.
🔁 RepetitionWe need action. We need it now.

Use real facts, or make it clear where a number comes from (“our class survey”). Never invent a fact and pretend it's official.

How to build a persuasive speech

1 🪝 HookA question, a fact or a short story.
2 💪 PointsOne point per paragraph, each backed up: a number, an example, a result.
3 🥊 Answer back“Some people say… However…”: deal with the other side, politely.
4 📣 Call to actionTell them exactly what to do: Sign… Join… Vote…

A speech talks to a live audience (“Good morning, everyone”, “you”, “Thank you for listening”). A letter to a newspaper or an article is written for readers, but the same techniques work.

Exam corner

Where this shows up in the exam

Writing tasks often ask you to persuade: a speech to your class or the council, a letter to a newspaper, an article or a blog post for teenagers. Reading questions ask how a writer tries to persuade the reader, so you need to name the techniques and explain their effect.

How marks are won and lost

  • Back up every point: an opinion with no reason or example wins few marks.
  • Name it, quote it, explain it: “The rhetorical question ‘Who wants…?’ makes the reader think…”
  • Answer the other side once: it shows you've thought it through.
  • Suit the reader: a speech to the council is formal, polite but firm. No insults.
  • End strongly with a clear call to action, not “That's all, thanks”.

Try these exam-style questions

Written by Léim in the style of the exam (not taken from past papers). "Where the marks would go" is our guide, not an official marking scheme.

  1. Name three persuasive techniques in this extract from a speech, and explain how one of them works.
    Good morning, everyone. Is a bin by the pier really too much to ask? Every Saturday, the pier is covered in cans, crisp bags and bottles. Our clean-up team filled 14 bags last month alone. You walk past it. Your little brother plays beside it.
    Show a model answer

    A rhetorical question (“Is a bin by the pier really too much to ask?”), the rule of three (“cans, crisp bags and bottles”), a statistic (“14 bags last month”) and direct address (“You walk past it. Your little brother…”). The statistic makes the problem sound real and proven: 14 bags in one month is hard to argue with.
    Where the marks would go: a mark for each technique named with a quote; the rest for explaining the effect on the listener.

  2. Write the opening of a speech to your school assembly: “Our school should have a phone-free lunchtime.” Use a rhetorical question and a statistic.
    Show a model answer

    Good morning, everyone. When did you last have a real conversation at lunchtime? In our class survey, 18 of 25 students said they spend most of lunch on their phones. I believe a phone-free lunchtime would give us back that hour.
    Where the marks would go: a greeting for the audience; a rhetorical question as a hook; a clear statistic with its source (“our class survey”); the point stated clearly.

  3. Someone says: “A phone-free lunchtime is unfair, because students need their phones to contact their parents.” Write two sentences that answer this point.
    Show a model answer

    Some people say students need their phones to contact their parents. However, parents can still ring the school office, and phones would only be in lockers for one hour.
    Where the marks would go: the other side's point stated fairly; a counter word (However); a reason that deals with THAT point; polite, no insults.

  4. Write the last paragraph of a speech to the county council asking for a skate park.
    Show a model answer

    A skate park would give young people a safe place to meet, keep skaters off busy roads and bring the town together. Please vote for the skate park at next month's meeting. Thank you for listening.
    Where the marks would go: the main points pulled together (here as a rule of three); a clear call to action (“Please vote for…”); a polite, formal close for this audience.
